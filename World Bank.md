## A NOTE ON HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT

The World Bank is a deceptively simple name for a genuinely complicated machine. It is five institutions under one roof, owned by 189 governments, run day to day by a 25-seat board of career diplomats, funded by a mixture of member-country subscriptions, donor gifts, loan repayments, and its own borrowing on global bond markets, and staffed by roughly seventeen thousand people spread across roughly 140 country offices. It committed $161.9 billion in a single recent year. It has lent money to nearly every country on earth, including countries fighting each other. It is, at once, the world's largest development lender, its largest development-data producer, the host of a dozen global funds, the manager of reconstruction money for at least three war zones, and — as of 2026 — a trustee of the most controversial peace-and-reconstruction instrument since the Iraq occupation.

This document tells that story in full. Part I explains where the Bank came from — the poker game at Bretton Woods in 1944, the forgotten power struggle of 1946–47 that actually determined what the institution would become, and the four decades in which its family of institutions was assembled. Part II is the owner's manual: how the machine works today, mechanism by mechanism, with every pipeline, formula, gate, and safeguard shown step by step. Part III is the human story: thirteen presidents, each in full. Part IV is the money: the complete numerical record of recent years. Part V is the casebook: every major scandal and documented corruption case, told with names, dates, and evidence status. Part VI extracts the seven structural patterns that connect eighty years of episodes. Part VII documents the institution as it stands in September 2026 — its reforms, its ruptures, and the Board of Peace crisis. Part VIII weighs the evidence for and against the institution's existence and lays out, in full, every serious reform blueprint on record.

Two ground rules apply throughout. First, every corruption claim carries a pedigree tag: **[CONVICTED]** means a court of law found it; **[ON TRIAL]** or **[INDICTED]** means charges are live and the presumption of innocence applies; **[ADMITTED]** means a guilty plea or negotiated admission; **[WB-SANCTIONED]** means the Bank's own administrative system found it (a lower standard than criminal proof); **[INTERNAL-FOUND]** means the Bank's own auditors, tribunal, or accountability body substantiated it; **[ALLEGED]** means credible reporting or official filings not yet tested in any forum. Second, nothing here is invented. Where the record is disputed, both sides are given. Where a proceeding is live, its status is stated as of September 29, 2026.

A glossary habit: whenever a technical term first appears, it is defined in plain words right there. Dollar figures are US dollars unless stated. Fiscal years (FY) run July 1 to June 30.

# PART I — ORIGINS AND BEFORE

## 1. The World the Bank Was Born Into (1933–1944)

To understand why the World Bank exists, you have to understand what the world looked like in July 1944. The Great Depression had shown that when global money flows seize up, governments turn on each other: competitive currency devaluations, tariff walls, beggar-thy-neighbor trade policies, and — eventually — the collapse of international order itself. The Second World War was still raging — the Normandy landings had happened less than a month before the conference opened — but the United States and Britain were already planning the peace. Their planners believed the Depression and the war shared a root cause: the collapse of international economic cooperation after the First World War. The remedy, articulated in the Atlantic Charter's 1941 economic clauses, was a system of permanent international economic institutions so that no country would again face crisis alone — and so that the next crisis would not become the next war.

Two men designed that system, and they disagreed about almost everything.

**John Maynard Keynes** — the most famous economist alive, a British Treasury adviser who had effectively invented macroeconomics — proposed an International Clearing Union: a genuinely international central bank that would create its own currency (the "bancor") and automatically recycle the trade surpluses of rich countries into investment in poor ones. In Keynes's design, the system would discipline creditors, not just debtors: a country running persistent surpluses would be charged, just as debtors were. It was elegant, radical, and dead on arrival. The United States — then the world's great creditor — was never going to accept a system designed to discipline it.

**Harry Dexter White**, a senior US Treasury official (assistant secretary, effectively the Treasury's chief international economist), drafted what the Americans actually wanted: a stabilization fund and a reconstruction bank, both capitalized by member subscriptions, which meant both governed in proportion to money contributed — which meant both controlled by the United States, which would hold by far the largest subscription. White won nearly every negotiation, partly because Britain was bankrupt and borrowing from America even as it negotiated. White's own story has a dark coda: in 1948 he was publicly accused of passing information to Soviet intelligence (the Venona intercepts and the "Silvermaster group" files remain contested to this day), he testified before the House Un-American Activities Committee, and he died of a heart attack in August 1948 — days after his testimony. He was, at his death, the first American executive director of the IMF.

The **Bretton Woods Conference** convened on July 1, 1944, at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire — 730 delegates from 44 countries, with delegations that included, among many others, the Chinese delegation headed by H.H. Kung, the Indian delegation led by A. Ramaswami Mudaliar with B.K. Nehru among its economists, the French delegation including Pierre Mendès France, and the Belgian finance minister Camille Gutt (who would go on to chair the IMF's first board). US Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr. presided; Keynes led Commission II (the Bank commission) despite being mortally ill. The conference ran three weeks, through July 22. Out of it came the **Final Act** and two institutions:

- The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** — to police the fixed exchange-rate system (currencies pegged to the US dollar, the dollar pegged to gold at $35 an ounce) and to lend short-term to countries with balance-of-payments crises.
- The **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD)** — the original World Bank — to finance the rebuilding of war-torn Europe and, secondarily, the development of poorer countries.

The design choices made in those three weeks echo to today:

- **Subscriptions, not bancors.** Each member subscribed capital in proportion to its economic size. The Articles prescribed $10 billion of initial capital; only 20 percent was ever expected to be paid in — and only 2 percent of the whole in gold or US dollars at the start. The remaining 80 percent was "callable": a standing promise to pay if the Bank ever needed it to meet obligations to its own creditors. That callable-capital architecture — the Bank's AAA credit rating resting on promises no member has ever had to keep — remains the machine's foundation. Its origins are older than the Bank: the model descends from the US National Bank Act of 1863 (which required only half of bank capital to be paid in), the Bank for International Settlements (1930), and a wartime inter-American bank proposal.
- **Weighted voting.** Voting power equaled subscription share. The United States opened with roughly a third of the votes and — because amending the Articles requires an 85 percent majority — an effective veto it retains to this day at roughly 15.9 percent.
- **The 85 percent rule.** Almost nothing in this document's later story — no reform, no admission, no punishment — can happen over an American objection. The rule that governs the rules can only be changed by the one country the rules most need to change.
- **Political neutrality, on paper.** Article IV restricts the Bank to economic considerations and bars interference in members' political affairs. Article VII grants sweeping legal immunities. Both articles matter enormously later.
- **Management over board.** Between July 15 and 19, 1944, the drafting texts quietly shifted control of callable capital from the board of directors to "the Bank" itself — i.e., management. Historians of the drafting mark it as the first of a thousand small decisions that made the World Bank a management-led institution.

The Soviet Union participated at Bretton Woods — its delegates reportedly dismissed the institutions as "branches of Wall Street" — and then never ratified. (Russia would join only in 1992; the Bank would stop lending to it after 2014 and it departed as a borrower entirely after 2022.) Cuba, by contrast, joined in 1946 and later withdrew (1964), one of only a handful of leavers in the institution's history.

## 2. The Forgotten Power Struggle: Savannah and the First Six Months (1945–1947)

The Articles came into force on December 27, 1945, with 21 founding members. The institution's inaugural meeting, at Savannah, Georgia, March 8–18, 1946, settled the question the Articles had left open: would the executive directors run the Bank day to day, or would management? The answer, reached in committee work that most histories skip, was management — and the conflict that produced it nearly killed the institution in its crib.

**Eugene Meyer** — the first president, from June 18, 1946 — was a genuine Wall Street founding figure: Lazard Frères partner, a New York Stock Exchange seat since 1901, a fortune made in copper and mining, Chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1930 to 1933, head of the wartime Reconstruction Finance Corporation, and, since 1933, owner of the Washington Post. He was Truman's idea of a safe pair of hands, recruited through Secretary of State Byrnes and Chief Justice Vinson. Meyer staffed the new institution carefully — Harold Smith as vice president, Chester McLain as general counsel, Charles Pineo running the loan department, with a young staff that included Leonard Rist (who would define its economics), Richard Demuth (its best connected diplomat-bureaucrat), Aron Broches (future ICSID architect), and Martin Rosen. He built the staff to roughly 150 people by December 1946.

And he lasted exactly six months, resigning December 4, effective December 18, 1946. The proximate cause was a power fight with the US executive director, Emilio Collado — a Treasury-backed official who believed the directors, not the president, should run operations. Meyer refused to submit the first loan applications until minimum standards were met; Washington wanted speed; and Meyer concluded the job was not worth the candle ("I'm too old for that" was the exit line). His daughter, Katharine Graham, would define the Washington Post's modern era. Three months later, on January 23, 1947, Harold Smith — the ablest administrator in the building — died suddenly. For nearly three months the Bank had no president. Collado chaired a caretaker board that no one wanted to make permanent.

Then the board turned to **John J. McCloy** — chairman of Chase National Bank, chairman of the Council on Foreign Relations, former Assistant Secretary of War, a man wired into every center of American power. McCloy drove the bargain that defines the institution to this day. He told the board, in essence: give me a clear declaration that management manages — the directors set policy, but recommendations come from management, and if the directors are not fully in agreement with that, "we would go on back to New York that afternoon." He brought **Robert L. Garner** from General Foods as vice president; **Eugene Black** moved from US executive director to the Bank's executive vice president, and the first bonds were prepared for market. A June 1947 Committee on Organization formalized the settlement: a president with genuine executive authority, and a resident board of directors whose job was oversight, not management. Every World Bank president since has governed in McCloy's shadow. When you read, later in this document, that the board "considers but does not manage" the loan pipeline, you are reading McCloy's ultimatum of 1947 still in force.

That same season, the Bank did the two things that set its character. It went to the bond markets — selling its first issues within months of opening, because unlike the IMF, the Bank would finance itself largely by borrowing, which meant it had to keep the confidence of Wall Street from its first day. (The first non-dollar borrowing came in 1947: a 2.5 percent Swiss franc serial issue of 4 million francs.) And it made its first loans: **$250 million to France**, signed May 9, 1947 — its signature operation, and one that required two dozen countries to waive the rule that no borrower could hold more than 10 percent of the portfolio; then the Netherlands and Denmark in 1947; then, in March 1948, its first unambiguously "development" loan — $13.5 million to Chile for agricultural machinery. McCloy himself was present at the creation of the Marshall Plan (announced June 1947); the Bank's relationship with the Plan was the shape of its whole future — asked to lend to Europe while Europe received American grants, simultaneously indispensable and bypassed.

The UN relationship agreement was completed in November 1947: the Bank became an "independent specialized agency" of the UN system under Articles 57 and 63 of the UN Charter, with a reciprocal promise that the UN would refrain from making recommendations on particular loans and that "action to be taken by the Bank on any loan is a matter to be determined by the independent exercise of the Bank's own judgment." The negotiations had been acrimonious through 1946–47 — the Bank feared political control and feared damage to its Wall Street credit rating; the UN side, led in the Economic and Social Council by Norwegian diplomat Finn Moe, attacked its demand for special privileges (the ECOSOC vote was 13–3, with the Soviet bloc against). The compromise language — still in force — became the Bank's permanent shield whenever anyone suggested it answer to anyone else.

## 3. The Black Era and the Aswan Shock (1949–1963)

**Eugene Black** — the Chase banker turned US executive director — became president on July 1, 1949, and ran the Bank for thirteen and a half years: the era when its operating culture was set. Under Black, lending grew from a trickle to a flow; the Bank's credit was established to the point that "Black's Bank" was a Wall Street commonplace; the 1959 general capital increase doubled subscribed capital; and the **International Finance Corporation** was created in 1956 — the Bank's private-sector arm, conceived in the early 1950s (a 1951 staff memo by Robert Garner) to invest in private companies in developing countries without government guarantees, on the theory that the missing middle of development finance was investable private enterprise. The IFC's Articles came into force on July 20, 1956, with authorized capital of $100 million; Garner, who had shepherded it, was its first president. It began operations in 1957.

The Black era's most famous episode ended in humiliation — and taught the institution its central lesson about politics.

**The Aswan Dam affair (1955–56).** Egypt under Gamal Abdel Nasser sought financing for the High Aswan Dam — the largest development project on earth at the time, intended to double Egypt's arable land and tame the Nile's floods. The Bank's own engineering assessment, delivered August 30, 1955, found the project technically and economically sound. On December 16, 1955, the United States and Britain offered a grant package of about $84 million (the US share $56–70 million depending on the account; Britain $14 million-plus) as the Western contribution; the Bank's $200 million loan was to follow, conditional on the grants materializing, competitive bidding, inflation control, and Egyptian financial discipline. On February 9, 1956, Black and Nasser issued a communiqué announcing "substantial agreement." The parties around the table had names: Ambassador Ahmed Hussein for Egypt, Finance Minister Abdel-Moneim El-Qaysouni, special envoy Robert Anderson (Eisenhower's oil troubleshooter), Assistant Secretary of State Herbert Hoover Jr., and a cast of intermediaries that included George V. Allen and Robert Murphy.

On July 19, 1956, Secretary of State John Foster Dulles abruptly withdrew the American offer — over Black's objections and, by Black's own later account, while the Bank president believed the deal was alive. Britain followed within days. A week later, on July 26, 1956, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal to finance the dam himself; in October, Britain, France, and Israel invaded Egypt; the crisis nearly ignited a superpower confrontation before the US forced its own allies to withdraw. The Soviet Union stepped in — a December 27, 1958 agreement committed 400 million rubles for the first stage — and the dam was completed in 1970, the year Nasser died. A US Senate (Fulbright) subcommittee later found no economic justification for Dulles's withdrawal; the decision had been geopolitical from the start, aimed at punishing Nasser's non-alignment.

The lesson every Bank president since has drawn: the Bank can assess economics, but politics sits above it — and its largest shareholder's politics sit above everything. The institution's defenders read Aswan as proof the Bank is a technocrat among sovereigns; its critics, as proof that the technocrat answers to Washington. Both are correct, which is precisely the point. (A coda that displays the Bank's pragmatism: in March 1960 it still lent Egypt $56 million for Suez Canal enlargement — a different project, a different politics.)

## 4. Building the Family: IDA, ICSID, MIGA (1960–1988)

Three more institutions were bolted onto the original Bank across three decades, each filling a gap its founders left:

**The International Development Association (IDA) — 1960.** The IBRD lent at near-market rates and only to creditworthy borrowers. The poorest countries could not afford it, and rich countries would not guarantee their loans. The compromise was championed in Congress by Senator Mike Monroney (whose 1958 resolution directed the US executive director to press for a soft-loan affiliate), studied by a National Advisory Council report in August 1959, and pushed through by the Eisenhower administration over the Treasury's doubts. IDA came into being on September 24, 1960, with 15 founding signatories: Australia, Canada, China, Germany, India, Italy, Malaysia, Norway, Pakistan, Sudan, Sweden, Thailand, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Vietnam. Its initial resources were $912.7 million. Operations began November 8, 1960; the first credit — $9 million for a western highway — went to Honduras on May 12, 1961; within its first eight months IDA had 51 members and had committed $101 million to Sudan, Chile, India, and Honduras. The replenishment rhythm (IDA1 in 1964: $750 million; see the full table in Part IV) created the institution's most distinctive ritual: every three years, donor officials gather as "IDA Deputies" to negotiate how much they will give and what policies their money buys. The US share of early replenishments was 42 percent; it would fall steadily to roughly a quarter by the mid-1970s and far lower in effective terms later. One structural peculiarity mattered from the start and matters still: IDA's money is a gift from rich countries but its governance votes sit in the same weighted system as the IBRD's — the donors' money, the borrowers' votes, and never the twain reconciled. By IDA's 2024 reckoning it had provided $533 billion since inception, and 46 countries had graduated from eligibility.

**The International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) — 1966.** As private foreign investment grew, so did disputes between foreign investors and host states. The Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States (the "Washington Convention") created ICSID as an impartial arbitration court; it entered into force on October 14, 1966. Aron Broches, the Bank's Dutch general counsel, was its architect. It is the least visible of the five arms and eventually one of the most consequential: by June 2025 it had administered 1,058 cases in total (347 in the year then ended — a record), with 158 contracting states and 8 further signatories. Its decisions can bankrupt countries: a 2022 award of $11 billion against Pakistan in the Reko Diq mining case is among the largest in legal history. Its critics call it a system of "corporate sovereignty"; its defenders note that investors who fear expropriation simply will not invest without it. Both can be true, and the docket proves both: mining (about 19 percent) and oil and gas (about 24–25 percent) dominate the caseload; roughly 62 percent of cases rest on bilateral investment treaties; investors from Western Europe file about 44 percent of new cases; and outcomes in recent years have upheld claims in about 57 percent of decided cases, rejected them in 24 percent, and declined jurisdiction in 19 percent. The annulment mechanism (an ad hoc committee of three, appointed by the Administrative Council's chairman — the World Bank president — from the Panel of Arbitrators) can vacate awards for improper constitution, manifest excess of powers, corruption, serious procedural departure, or failure to state reasons, within 120 days (three years where corruption is the ground).

**The Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA) — 1988.** Political-risk insurance — covering investors against expropriation, war, currency inconvertibility, and breach of contract — had been proposed in the 1950s, studied by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and the UN Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in the 1960s, and shelved repeatedly; a mid-1970s attempt died with the Bank's capital constraints. Under president Barber Conable, general counsel Ibrahim Shihata (the Bank's Saudi-born Egyptian lawyer, general counsel from 1983 and the intellectual force of the institution's legal expansion) drove the MIGA Convention through: a Committee of the Whole met twenty times between June and September 1985; the Convention was approved September 12, 1985, and opened for signature in Seoul on October 11, 1985. It entered into force on April 12, 1988 — the threshold being ratification by the United States and Britain, whose signatures arrived that spring — and the first board met June 22, 1988. Yoshio Teresawa of Japan was its first executive vice president. A 2010 amendment (effective November 14, 2010) expanded eligible investments. By fiscal 2025 MIGA had issued about $94 billion in guarantees across more than a thousand projects in 124 countries — and in recent years it has become central to the Bank's Ukraine response, co-insuring war risk with the US International Development Finance Corporation (a June 2023 agreement, fifteen-year coverage), Poland's export credit agency KUKE (2024), and the war-risk frameworks built with donor backing since 2022.

By 1988, the family was complete: IBRD (loans to middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries), IDA (concessional finance for the poorest), IFC (private sector equity and loans), MIGA (insurance), and ICSID (investment arbitration) — five institutions, one headquarters, one staff, one president. The five share ownership but not balance sheets: IBRD and IDA are the "Bank" proper; IFC, MIGA, and ICSID have their own charters, their own capital, and — formally — their own boards, though in practice the same 25 people sit on all of them.

This part is the owner's manual, mechanism by mechanism. ## 5. The Five Arms and Their Jobs

| Arm | Founded | Legal charter | What it does | Where the money comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IBRD** | 1944 | Bretton Woods Articles | Loans, guarantees, crisis instruments for middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries | Global bond markets, backed by member subscriptions (mostly callable) |
| **IDA** | 1960 | IDA Articles | Concessional loans and grants to the poorest countries | Donor replenishments (3-year cycle), loan reflows, own bond issuance since 2018, IBRD/IFC transfers |
| **IFC** | 1956 | IFC Articles | Equity, loans, funds, and advisory for private companies in developing countries | Own capital, retained earnings, borrowing; merged its Asset Management Company into itself in January 2020 |

The legal separateness matters: the IFC can be sued only under its own Articles (which is why *Jam v. IFC* went to the US Supreme Court, not to any Bank process), and ICSID is not even a lender — it is a court. But governance is unified: one president, four boards of directors sharing the same 25 people, one staff of roughly 17,000–18,000, one headquarters (1818 H Street NW, Washington, D.C., in a main complex designed by Kohn Pedersen Fox, with the J Building at 701 18th Street held on a 99-year ground lease from 1983 and purchased outright for $165 million in September 2025, and the G Building at 1776 G Street bought for $129.5 million in December 2019 — a tax-exempt real-estate empire, since the Bank's Article VII immunity from taxation costs the District of Columbia roughly $800,000 a year on the J Building land alone).

## 6. Ownership and Voting: Who Actually Controls It

**The Board of Governors** — one governor per member country, usually the finance or development minister — is the supreme organ under the Articles. It meets formally once a year (at the Annual Meetings) and holds all the powers it never uses: admitting members, amending the Articles, electing executive directors in the gaps, confirming the capital increases. Real power sits with the **25-member Board of Executive Directors**, resident at headquarters, meeting Tuesday and Thursday mornings of every working week.

The 25 seats are **constituencies**, not countries. The United States, Japan, China, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom each appoint their own director; the other 183 members share the remaining seats in multi-country groups — meaning a single director speaks for twenty-three African countries in the Africa Group 2 seat while America speaks for itself alone. A 2023 snapshot of the table: the American director Lailee Moghtader (whose office staff of senior advisors and analysts is a Treasury outpost in miniature); Germany's Martina Metz chairing the European Union constituency; the UK's Robin Tasker heading a Nordic-Baltic-British group; India's Aparna Subramani and (for a separate India-centered group) Chandra Sekhar Mohapatra; China's director; Japan's; Saudi Arabia's; and the African-group directors Anne Kabagambe (Africa Group 2) and Jean-Claude Tchatchouang (Africa Group 1). Voting power mirrors the money: as of mid-2023 the US held 15.90 percent of IBRD votes (439,294 of them), Japan 7.26 percent, China 5.64 percent, Germany 4.32 percent, France and the United Kingdom 3.96 percent each; in IDA the American share is 9.70 percent, Japan 8.39 percent, the UK 6.94 percent. The chair of the smallest constituency presides over about 1.4 percent.

**Why America can say no to anything.** Amending the Articles takes an 85 percent majority. With 15.9 percent, the US can block any amendment — including every proposed amendment of the voting system itself. This is the structural lock at the heart of the institution: the rule that governs the rules can only be changed by the one country the rules most need to change.

**How the board actually decides.** The president chairs the board and votes only to break a tie; there is no vice-chair in the chair's absence — the dean of the board presides. Formally, decisions require a simple majority of votes cast and a quorum of a majority of directors holding at least half the voting power; abstentions do not count as votes. In practice, the board almost never votes. It operates by **consensus**, or more precisely by a system of negotiated silence: an item circulated as "absence of objection" (AOB) is adopted unless a director speaks against it — and silence, once the meeting closes, is final and cannot be revisited. Written statements are due two working days before the meeting; items objected to are "pulled" into discussion; the Tuesday/Thursday rhythm processes dozens of items a week, the great majority by AOB without any debate at all. Researchers who study the board call it "delegate-and-control": consensus works because the directors with the big vote shares quietly assure themselves of each other's positions beforehand (the informal count), so the public record shows harmony while the private negotiations carry the weight. A formal vote, when one is forced, can be carried by as little as 18 percent of the membership — the math of a system in which 160-plus members have entrusted their votes to 25 hands.

The board's standing committees (each with a quorum of four, two-year terms, a convention that the chair from a borrowing country pairs with a vice-chair from a non-borrowing one): the **Audit Committee** (external audit, internal audit, risk oversight; the FY2024–26 roster chaired by Abdulaziz Almulla); the **Budget Committee** (the institution's money; chaired by Zainab Ahmed, the former Nigerian finance minister); the **Committee on Development Effectiveness** (CODE — evaluations, safeguards, the Inspection Panel; chaired by Parameswaran Iyer, architect of India's Clean India program); the **Committee on Governance and Executive Directors' Administrative Matters** (COGAM — the board's own affairs and corporate governance); the **Human Resources Committee**; the **Ethics Committee**; and the all-directors **Steering Committee** meeting every two months as the primary forum where directors press management between meetings. A small secretariat — the Corporate Secretary, currently Aradhana Kumar-Capoor, who also serves as Executive Secretary to the Development Committee — keeps the paper moving.

The executive directors' offices are themselves a small civil service: the Africa Group 1 office runs an executive director, an alternate, roughly ten senior advisors and advisors rotating across twenty-two countries, plus administrative staff and nationals seconded under the Voice Secondment Program. The offices' combined budget is about $85 million a year — nearly twice the Independent Evaluation Group's.

**The Development Committee** — formally the "Joint Ministerial Committee of the Boards of Governors of the Bank and the Fund on the Transfer of Real Resources to Developing Countries," created in 1974 to give finance ministers a steering wheel after the collapse of the Bretton Woods exchange system — is the bridge between the board and political masters: twenty-five ministers (usually finance or development), meeting twice a year at the Spring Meetings and the Annual Meetings, issuing a communiqué that is the closest thing the institution has to a political directive. The chair rotates among members, assisted by the Executive Secretary (the Bank's Corporate Secretary). The lineage of chairs tells its own story: Yashwant Sinha of India (2000–01), Trevor Manuel of South Africa (2001–05), Alberto Carrasquilla of Colombia (2005–07), Agustín Carstens of Mexico (2007–09), Ibrahim Al Khalifa of Bahrain (2009–11), Leszek Belka of Poland (2011–15), Sri Mulyani Indrawati of Indonesia (2016–18), Ken Ofori-Atta of Ghana (2018–20), Mia Mottley of Barbados (2020–21), Danilo Astori of Uruguay (2021–22), then Mohammed Al Hussaini of the UAE (2022–24), then Elisabeth Svantesson of Sweden (October 2024– ). On April 16, 2026, at the 113th meeting, the committee failed to issue a communiqué **or even a chair's statement for the first time in its history** — a rupture detailed in Part VII.

**Choosing the president.** Formally: the executive directors appoint the president, by consensus, from candidates nominated by governors (anyone may nominate through their director; candidates must be nationals of member countries and may not be sitting governors or directors). The 2011 Selection Principles added criteria (leadership of a large organization, public-sector familiarity, communication ability, commitment to development) and a nominating window of about five weeks; a shortlist of no more than three emerges through informal soundings; the dean of the board chairs the process. The tradition since 1946 has been the American candidate: McCloy, Black, Woods, McNamara, Clausen (with William Simon briefly in between, a rare case of a nominee withdrawn for lack of support), Conable, Preston, Wolfensohn (Australian-born but American-backed), Wolfowitz, Zoellick, Kim, Malpass, Banga. Europe picks the IMF's managing director under the reciprocal "gentlemen's agreement." The record: 2012 was the first contested selection (Kim versus Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala of Nigeria and José Antonio Ocampo of Colombia); 2016 Kim was the only candidate within hours (forty-two former senior officials publicly protested); 2023 Banga was unopposed, confirmed May 3, taking office June 2. In April 2026 Brazil formally proposed replacing the system with a **double-majority election** — a majority of voting power *and* a majority of member countries — the first structural challenge to the appointment privilege in the institution's history.

## 7. The Money: Where $150+ Billion a Year Comes From

### 7.1 IBRD: Borrowing on the World's Faith

The IBRD is, at heart, a highly unusual bank. Its usable equity is modest — $54–67 billion across recent fiscal years — yet it holds a **triple-A credit rating** from Moody's (Aaa, affirmed with a stable outlook in February 2025), S&P (AAA, December 2025), and Fitch (AAA). The rating rests on three pillars: a near-perfect repayment record (in eighty years, the Bank says it has never lost a penny of contractual principal or interest, except in two still-outstanding cases — Zimbabwe and Belarus), a conservative leverage policy (the equity-to-loans ratio: a policy floor of 20 percent until 2024, then 19, then 18 — meaning roughly five-plus dollars of loans for every dollar of equity), and its **callable capital** — about $233 billion of subscribed-but-uncalled member commitments, with the top ten callable providers being the United States (16.4 percent of the total), Japan, China, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, India, Russia, Saudi Arabia, and Italy.

Callable capital has never been called — not once, at any major development bank, ever — and economists argue about whether it is really capital at all. The honest description (from the Overseas Development Institute's research): a specialized guarantee for creditors, ring-fenced; under the capital-increase resolutions even the portion unlocked for "direct use" (about 13 percent, $41.6 billion) may only be tapped when actually required to meet obligations, never for lending. The G20 major-economies' capital-adequacy review found the processes "not well understood" even among members. But the psychological effect is real: bond investors buy World Bank paper treating it as backed by the treasuries of its members — which is why the Bank's first-ever "Enhanced Callable Capital" contribution (New Zealand, January 2026, $50 million) was treated as a landmark.

The treasury operation is industrial in scale: the Bank is among the world's largest supranational issuers, running a Global Medium-Term Note program and benchmark bonds in dollars, euros, and other currencies, with maturities out to 35 years (50 years for global-challenge cross-border projects). Its paper enjoys 0 percent risk weighting under Basel rules and qualifies as top-tier central-bank collateral at the Federal Reserve, the ECB, the Bank of England, the Swiss National Bank, the Reserve Bank of Australia, and others — the market treats it as quasi-sovereign. Borrowings run $50–60 billion a year (FY2024: $52.4 billion of medium- and long-term issues; the borrowing portfolio around $287 billion). And it pioneered the labeled market: the world's first green bond in 2008; social bonds, SDG bonds, and outcome bonds since — including the 2022 Wildlife Conservation Bond (the "Rhino Bond," $150 million, returns tied to rhino population growth in South African reserves) and Amazon reforestation bonds whose coupons depend on verified carbon. The spread between what the Bank pays to borrow and what it charges borrowers is the machine's fuel: loan interest covered net administrative expenses fully in FY2024 and FY2025 — the budget, in effect, is paid by borrowers.

What it charges: a contractual spread of 0.50 percent over its funding cost, a maturity premium of 0–0.20 percent, a market-risk premium for fixed-spread loans (10–15 basis points), a front-end fee of 0.25 percent, and a commitment fee of 0.25 percent on undisbursed balances. Single-country borrowing limits apply (exceptions for the giants: India $18.5 billion, Indonesia $18.5 billion, China and Mexico $17 billion, Brazil $16.5 billion, each carrying a 50-basis-point surcharge above the threshold). Special products price higher: single-currency and currency-pool loans with derivatives, development-policy deferred-drawdown options at 0.25 percent front-end plus 0.50 percent standby fees, catastrophe drawdown options at 0.50 percent front-end plus renewal fees. Waivers for the poorest borrowers cost the Bank only millions a year.

The balance sheet in a recent year (FY2024): loans outstanding $263 billion; allocable income $1.85 billion, of which $515 million went to IDA, $834 million to general reserves, $500 million to surplus; net income $2.14 billion. The income statement's quiet miracle: after eighty years the Bank still earns its keep the way McCloy set it up — on the spread.

### 7.2 IDA: The Donor Ritual and the Quiet Transformation

IDA's money comes from four sources, and their proportions tell the story of its transformation. **Donor replenishments** supplied almost everything at the start; today they supply roughly a quarter. **Loan reflows** — repayments of past credits — fund the majority of the envelope. IDA first borrowed on capital markets in April 2018 ($1.5 billion five-year bond at 2.75 percent, five times oversubscribed; benchmarks in dollars, euros, sterling, Swedish kronor, and Norwegian kroner followed; a $4 billion five-year at 4.375 percent in June 2024 with BNP Paribas, Nomura, TD and others as leads), carrying the same AAA/Aaa ratings as its sister. And the IBRD and IFC transfer surplus income (IBRD: $515 million in FY2024; IFC: cumulative transfers approaching $3.9 billion). Newer leverage instruments: the **hybrid capital** framework (a perpetual instrument with debt-and-equity features, modeled on the African Development Bank's September 2025 design — no step-up coupons, a five-year reset, discretionary non-cumulative coupons, mandatory cancellation if total assets exceed 7.5 times paid-in capital plus reserves, principal loss-absorption if callable capital is ever called, deeply subordinated; IDA's IDA21 framework extends eligibility to philanthropies) and a portfolio-guarantee platform under which shareholders' guarantees (first-loss) stretch every donor dollar further.

The replenishment process is a masterpiece of ritual. Every three years: four to five formal meetings of "IDA Deputies" (one senior official per donor plus borrower representatives, with civil-society consultations on the side) over roughly a year. Meeting one selects the special themes; middle meetings haggle over the allocation formula, grant shares, and financing framework; the final pledging meeting — IDA21's was in South Korea, December 5–6, 2024 — is where donors announce numbers and sign the deputies' report; then each parliament must ratify, the US Congress historically the slowest (with "advance contributions" bridging the gap, a practice dating to the second replenishment, when eleven donors advanced $244.5 million to cover the American delay). **Who gets IDA, and how much, is a formula.** The Performance-Based Allocation (PBA) system computes each country's Country Performance Rating (CPR) from the **Country Policy and Institutional Assessment (CPIA)** — sixteen criteria in four clusters: (A) economic management (monetary and exchange-rate policy, fiscal policy, debt policy); (B) structural policies (trade, financial sector, business regulation); (C) social inclusion and equity (gender equality, equity of public-resource use, human-resource development, social protection and labor, environmental sustainability); (D) public-sector management and institutions (property rights and rule-based governance, quality of budgetary and financial management, efficiency of revenue mobilization, quality of public administration, transparency-accountability-and-corruption). The formula: CPR = 0.24×Cluster A + 0.68×Cluster D + 0.08×portfolio-performance rating; allocation then rises with CPR to the third power, population to the first power, and falls with gross national income (GNI) per capita to a power, plus a base allocation (raised from about $1.5 million to $15 million SDR — roughly $20 million — at IDA18), subject to caps, small-state exceptions, the FCV envelope (roughly 40 percent of IDA now flows to fragile and conflict states), debt-distress set-asides, the GROW window, and regional set-asides. The effect: Ethiopia, Nigeria, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and the DRC perennially top the list — Ethiopia received $3.9 billion in the FY26 allocation year. The CPIA's country scores remain secret (only cluster averages are published) — a transparency gap that critics have attacked for decades and that the allocation formula's legitimacy depends on.

**IDA terms** have hardened as reflows replaced gifts: credits ran up to 50 years in the earliest era (through 1987); 35- and 40-year terms followed; 25/40-year menus in 2011–14; and under IDA21 (effective July 1, 2025): standard credits of 31 years with a 6-year grace period at about 4 percent repayment from year 7 (roughly 1.3 percent all-in cost in dollars in late 2026), 40- and 50-year options for the poorest, small-economy terms of 40 years/10 grace, blend terms of 25 years/5 grace, and grants only for countries in debt distress (about a quarter of the envelope). Service charge 0.75 percent; commitment fee nil; front-end fee 0.25 percent; guarantee fee 0.75 percent. An acceleration clause doubles principal payments for creditworthy borrowers whose income per capita stays above the eligibility threshold.

### 7.3 The Trust-Fund Empire and the Rise of the FIF

Beyond its own balance sheets, the Bank administers a parallel universe: **trust funds** — money that belongs to donors, not to the Bank. The count peaked at 748 active trust funds in 2011 (about 12 percent of IBRD/IDA disbursements then, roughly $17 billion over 2007–11); the architecture persists at scale today: a three-level hierarchy (trustee-level agreement with each donor separately, then program windows, then disbursements to recipients via grant agreements), a mandatory checking against the debarment list, and a fee schedule: a $35,000 set-up fee per fund, an administration fee around 2 percent of contributions, and program-management fees up to 10.7 percent of actual costs (a "hybrid" scale now charges 5 percent of the first $50 million, 4 percent of the next $450 million, 3 percent of the next $500 million, 2 percent thereafter). Trust-fund accounting is **modified cash basis** — contributions are called electronically through the Development Partner Center portal, converted to a holding currency, commingled and invested — deliberately not full accrual accounting. Recipient-executed grants dominate (94 percent of trust-fund disbursements in FY2023; some funds finance governments, others NGOs and UN agencies directly); bank-executed trust funds carry an indirect-cost rate of 17 percent of personnel costs. Sunset exists on paper: an end-disbursement date, a completion report within six months, unspent balances returned pro-rata to donors — but funds are immortal in practice: donors roll them, and the Bank keeps the fees. Criticism is structural (a British Medical Journal series; UK aid-watchdog findings): earmarking bends research priorities, country programs, and even procurement patterns toward donor preferences — one documented extreme being donor-country infrastructure trust funds whose resources flow preferentially to contractors from that donor country.

The more formal species dominates the landscape now: **Financial Intermediary Funds (FIFs)** — global funds the Bank hosts as trustee, sometimes also as implementing agency, secretariat host, or combination. The roster as of 2025–26 ran to about two dozen: the Global Environment Facility (Bank trustee since 1991, one of its original implementing agencies alongside UNDP and UNEP; the GEF's own secretariat sits inside the Bank and its staff work under Bank rules — a fusion of identities that critics flag; GEF-8 in 2022 closed at a record $5.33 billion from 29 donors with the top five donors supplying 80–85 percent); the Green Climate Fund (interim trustee since 2010 at the UN climate conference's invitation, permanent from April 2019, renewed February 2023; a legally independent fund with its own 24-member board — twelve developed, twelve developing — co-chaired in rotation, secretariat in Songdo, and "accredited entities" through which money flows, including the Bank itself, a dual role that board members have flagged as a conflict of interest; UNDP is the largest implementing partner, the Bank a mid-sized one); the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (conceived at the Bank in 2000–01 — Jeffrey Sachs drafted it, Wolfensohn and Gates incubated it — hosted at the Bank at its 2002 birth with the Bank as treasury manager, where it remains, including running the International Finance Facility for Immunisation that has raised over $10 billion for Gavi on the bond market since 2006; the Global Fund has its own legal personality and moved its secretariat to Geneva); the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations; the Pandemic Fund (created 2022 as a financial-intermediary fund hosted at the Bank after the PEF's failure; governed by a board with a developing-country majority); the Afghanistan Reconstruction Trust Fund; the FORTIS Ukraine fund; the Loss and Damage Fund (interim trustee and secretariat host for four years from 2024; a 26-member board with a 14-seat developing-country majority including seats for the least-developed countries (LDCs) and small island developing states (SIDS), co-chaired initially by Richard Sherman of South Africa and Jean-Christophe Donnellier of France, legal personality seated with the Philippines; pledges of roughly $730 million against unquantified need, first disbursements planned around an initial "readiness" phase, and a Bank cost-recovery charge of 20.4 percent on direct costs — about 17 percent of the fund's total administrative budget in its first half-year — that critics call steep); the Global Financing Facility; the Global Agriculture and Food Security Program; the FCPF carbon funds; the PROBLUE and PROGREEN ocean and forest programs; the Global Infrastructure Facility (a G20 initiative of 2014 whose project-preparation pipeline has mobilized multiples of its small budget); and the UN-hosted FIF on Tax (launched at COP30 in Belém, 2025). FY2024 saw $10.6 billion contributed to FIFs through the Bank, with the Global Fund ($4.5 billion), Green Climate Fund ($1.6 billion), and GEF ($1.1 billion) the largest. The Bank's role as the world's default financial plumber — receive, hold, invest, transfer, report — has made "trustee" a second identity as important as "bank," and it is the identity most exposed in the Gaza crisis of Part VII.

### 7.4 The Budget and the Staff

The administrative budget tells you what the institution values. FY2025's net IBRD/IDA-funded budget was $3.5 billion ($5.1 billion all funds including trust-fund-executed work and external financing), with staff salaries and benefits consuming $2.9 billion — about 59 percent; short-term consultants $527 million; travel $349 million; contractual services $564 million. A standing mechanism diverts 5 percent of the budget annually into a productivity-savings pool ($144 million in FY2025) that funds new priorities — the internal fuel of the reform cycles of Part VI. Evolution-priority earmarks within it: global challenge programs, crisis preparedness, FCV, digital systems, the scorecard. The budget is approved annually by the board within a ±2 percent flexibility band; the Budget Committee endorses; corporate contingency and institutional escalators pad it.

In FY2025 the Bank employed roughly 18,000 staff (about 36 percent from the Americas, 24 percent from Europe, 13 percent from Africa at headquarters — a diversity deficit the Voice Secondment Program nibbles at), about a quarter of them short-term consultants — the shadow workforce that became a live scandal when an internal fraud case exposed systemic misuse of consultant contracts across dozens of projects and three countries, and which the 2026 reorganization then froze entirely (Part VII). Grades run GA to GK (top of the ladder roughly $491,000); the Young Professionals Program — the talent pipeline for future leadership — enters at grade GF (roughly $82,000–$151,000 plus benefits worth 35–70 percent more) with three eight-month rotations across operations and specialized functions; end-year evaluation runs against business objectives plus core competencies ("Create, Apply and Share Knowledge" among them); promotion requires both competence and business need. The president's total compensation (net-of-tax salary $585,790 plus benefits of $293,279 in FY23) exceeds that of any world leader; executive directors earn around $273,000 (alternates $236,000); senior vice presidents $409–475,000. Executive directors' offices cost about $85 million a year; the Independent Evaluation Group, the institution's conscience, about $44 million — the asymmetry is worth a moment's thought.

The staff retirement system, incidentally, is its own small empire: the Staff Retirement Plan (created 1948, made a trust in 2001) holds on the order of $20–50 billion in assets for roughly 15,000 active staff and 9,000 retirees; the 2009–10 "Net Plan" reform — a mandatory 5 percent contribution toward a 70 percent replacement target, blending a defined-benefit floor with a cash-balance top — was the institution's fiercest internal compensation fight in decades.

## 8. The Project Cycle: How an Idea Becomes Money

Everything the IBRD and IDA do flows through a standardized pipeline — the project cycle, six steps long:

**Step 1 — Identification.** The Bank and the borrowing government agree on what to do, drawing on the country's own strategy and the Bank's **Systematic Country Diagnostic** (SCD) — a study of a country's binding constraints, drawing on the institution's analytical work and consultations with parliaments, civil society, unions, the private sector, faith groups, and academics (some consultations now run online). The SCD feeds the four-year **Country Partnership Framework** (CPF), the joint business plan that sets out what the Bank will finance and why it has comparative advantage. The CPF is board-approved; the borrower government is consulted but does not approve it — a sovereignty nuance that bothers some governments more than others.

**Step 2 — Preparation.** The borrower (not the Bank — this is fundamental) prepares the project: feasibility studies, engineering, environmental and social screening, procurement planning, land and resettlement planning. The Bank supervises and can hire consultants to help, but the project is the borrower's. Preparation is where World Bank projects most often die of old age; three to four years from idea to approval is normal, and many never arrive. The Bank's 2023–25 simplification drive cut its *own* internal preparation steps from nineteen to twelve months.

**Step 3 — Appraisal.** The Bank's task team goes through everything — technical soundness, a cost-benefit economic analysis, fiduciary risk, environmental and social risk — and produces an appraisal package for the board. Quality-at-entry is overseen by a corporate review function whose existence is a direct descendant of the 1992 Wapenhans crisis (Part V).

**Step 4 — Negotiation and Board approval.** The borrower and the Bank negotiate the loan agreement's covenants — the binding promises. Then the package goes to the board under the consensus machinery: circulated in advance, adopted by silence unless a director objects, or debated and decided by vote if pulled. Directors' written statements go into the project file; the borrower is not in the room.

**Step 5 — Effectiveness.** The loan becomes a legal obligation only when stated conditions are met — typically the borrower has adopted an implementation plan, a project unit is staffed, counterpart funding assured, and sometimes a prior action (a law, a regulation) is on the books. (This is where Bangladesh's pipeline famously clogs: billions of dollars of approved projects sit unsigned or unmet for years.)

**Step 6 — Implementation, supervision, completion.** The borrower implements; the Bank supervises through Implementation Status and Results Reports (ISRs) roughly every six months, rating progress and outcomes on a six-point scale (highly satisfactory to highly unsatisfactory). At completion the borrower files an Implementation Completion Report, and the Independent Evaluation Group audits a sample and independently evaluates the largest projects. The cycle closes with lessons feeding back into Step 1 — in theory; in practice, as Part VI shows, the lessons repeatedly fail to carry.

### 8.1 The Procurement Gate

Money becomes roads only through procurement, and procurement is where the Bank's real power lies — over the borrower. The current framework (the Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers, July 2016, with the Standard Procurement Documents mandatory from March 2025): the borrower prepares a **Project Procurement Strategy for Development** (PPSD) — an analysis of market conditions, risks, and the best contracting approach — which the Bank reviews. The Bank then issues "no-objection" at each consequential step: the general and specific procurement notices, prequalification documents, bidding documents, evaluation reports, and award recommendations. Below risk-based thresholds the Bank reviews after the fact; above them, before. Contracts must be advertised publicly (on the UN Development Business platform and the Bank's own within two weeks of no-objection); awards carry a standstill period with automatic debriefing; a complaints process (Annex III) allows losing bidders to challenge; rated criteria are mandatory for international procurements since 2025. Every evaluation must check bidders against the **debarment list**; contracts with listed firms are prohibited; the Bank can declare misprocurement and cancel the loan share of a tainted contract, bar force-account use without justification, and cap variation orders (beyond 15 percent requires no-objection). Escalation inside the Bank runs from the task team leader through the Accredited Practice Manager to the Chief Procurement Officer; a high-value Operations Procurement Review Committee decides the biggest cases; processing standards promise seven business days per step. Fraud and corruption provisions (Regulation 1.16) feed the sanctions system; sexual-exploitation-and-abuse vetting can disqualify.

Since 2022 the apparatus runs through **STEP** (Systematic Tracking of Exchanges in Procurement), an online system covering the full chain — procurement plan, no-objection, bid management, evaluation, award, and contract management (the Contract Management Module launched October 2022) — which the Bank's own evaluator found arduous, manually entered, and disconnected from its financial systems. Composition note for Part V: in fiscal 2020, firms from China won 23.6 percent of contract value; the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) finds China accounts for roughly 3 percent of contracts by count but about 29 percent by dollars (its data limitation: borrower-identified registration ignores beneficial ownership).

### 8.2 How the Money Physically Moves

Disbursement mechanics are designed so the Bank never writes checks to contractors directly. Instead: the borrower opens a **Designated Account** at its central bank or a commercial bank, sized to roughly three months of expenditures; the borrower submits withdrawal applications against expense categories listed in the disbursement letter (some categories eligible for advances, others for reimbursement or direct payment); the Bank replenishes the account; the borrower's project unit pays suppliers. For lower-risk items the borrower can use **Statement of Expenditures** (SOE) — essentially self-certification of eligible spending with post-review, spot audits, and ex-post verification. Annual audited financial statements and management letters are mandatory; audit noncompliance triggers an escalating ladder (at four months: refusal of closing-date extensions, delays on new loans, withholding of advances, discontinuation of SOE-based disbursement; at nine months plus material internal-control weaknesses: suspension). Special commitments (letters of credit) can be issued for large contracts; in designated-account arrangements the commercial bank's role is that of a paying agent, not a fiduciary.

### 8.3 The Instrument Menu

- **Investment Project Financing (IPF)** — the classic project loan described above; roughly $17.5 billion of IBRD's FY2025 commitments.
- **Development Policy Financing (DPL)** — budget support: money straight into the treasury against a "policy matrix." The conditionality has three gears, and the distinction matters: **prior actions** (must be implemented before board approval — hard conditions; Ukraine's 2024 SOE law and Indonesia's 2025 licensing, foreign-direct-investment, and tax measures are recent examples); **tranche release conditions** (for multi-tranche operations); and **triggers** (for programmatic series — described expectations for the *next* operation, deliberately kept out of the legal agreement so they can flex). The apparatus exists to lend fast against reform promises — and to take the country to task if promises break.
- **Program-for-Results (PforR)** — financing a government's existing expenditure program, disbursing against **Disbursement-Linked Indicators** (DLIs): average design eight DLIs (range 3–12), about a third verified by independent government agencies or contracted verifiers, disbursement formulas agreed up front (pass/fail, linear, or capped, some scalable), a mid-term review, an expenditure reconciliation at closing (if the government spent less than it received, it must repay the difference), and fiduciary/E&S reliance on country systems. Exclusions: high E&S impact activities and works above OPRC thresholds.
- **Catastrophe-related instruments**: the **CERC** (Contingent Emergency Response Component, embedded in projects, activated by government declaration of emergency plus Bank no-objection against a Positive List of eligible activities — 137 activations worth $3.2 billion between 2012 and 2023, more than half for health emergencies (COVID), 39 percent in fragile states; a pooled variant aggregates resources across projects under central coordination, as in Sri Lanka); the **Cat DDO** (a standby credit line triggered by a formal state-of-emergency declaration — natural disaster or public-health emergency — up to $1 billion or 0.5 percent of GDP for IBRD ($500M/1 percent/ min $40M for IDA), a three-year drawdown period renewable up to four times (fifteen years maximum), approval conditional on an adequate macro framework and a satisfactory disaster-risk-management program; Grenada drew $20 million within weeks of Hurricane Beryl in July 2024 alongside a $55.5 million parametric payout, and a disaster clause in its 2015 restructured bond deferred payments; Nepal's triggers cover both natural disasters and public-health emergencies under its 2018 Public Health Service Act; Fiji's runs through Cabinet declarations under its Natural Disaster Management Act); the **Crisis Preparedness and Response Toolkit** (CPRT — triggerable credit lines plus pre-arranged emergency components, now used in some seventy-four countries); and **scalable DPOs** (up to 100 percent increase) with a Rapid Response Option top-up.
- **Guarantees** — the Bank guarantees private lenders against project or policy risk (fees roughly 50–70 basis points); MIGA sells political-risk insurance; the **WBG Guarantee Platform** (launched July 2024 at MIGA) issued $12.3 billion in FY2025 (MIGA $9.5B + IFC $1.2B + IBRD/IDA $1.6B) toward a $20 billion-a-year-by-2030 ambition. Notable anomaly: partial credit guarantees have been called exactly once in their history.
- **The carbon and cat-bond shelf**: green/social/SDG bonds; outcome bonds (rhino, Amazon); catastrophe bonds the Bank arranges for countries — the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility's (CCRIF's) first note in 2014 ($30M; the Bank supplied technical leadership and Japan the grant capital; Munich Re co-structured, Swiss Re advised), Mexico's 2009/2012 notes ($1.45B of cover in total), Jamaica's 2021 note ($185M) which paid out fully ($150M) after Hurricane Melissa in October 2025, and CAR 137 in May 2026 ($200M, three-plus-one seasons, AIR Worldwide as calculation agent, Aon and Swiss Re structuring, a coupon of the overnight-dollar benchmark rate plus a 12bp funding margin and a 675bp risk margin, listed on the Singapore Exchange — the investor base two-thirds insurance-linked-securities funds); plus the 2017 Pandemic Emergency Financing Facility (the "pandemic bond" — $500M of cover ($425M insurance window, $50–64M cash), whose COVID payout conditions were met only partially and late in April–May 2020 (~$196M), after which the instrument was allowed to expire in April 2021 and replaced by the Pandemic Fund).

### 8.4 When a Borrower Stops Paying

The arrears cascade is codified in the Loan Handbook: reminder five days after a missed payment, second reminder at fifteen; notice at thirty and thirty-five and forty-five; at fifty days the guarantor loses its partial interest waiver on guaranteed loans; at fifty-three the Bank informs co-financiers and regional development banks; at sixty days **suspension of disbursements takes effect** and the board is notified; at ninety days cancellation of undisbursed balances is recommended; six months of arrears (a second consecutive missed payment) triggers formal notice to the member country and the board within two days, the "trigger date," possible public disclosure, and **nonaccrual status** — the Bank stops recognizing income and the world is told; more than six months appears in the financial statements. Audit-covenant noncompliance runs its own ladder (four months: sanctions; nine months: suspension). Reinstatement requires curing and restoring advances.

The track record: twenty-seven nonaccrual events since 1947 (the first in 1985), peaking at nine countries simultaneously (end-FY1989 and end-FY2001), averaging under one percent of the portfolio annually, concentrated in Africa (thirteen events). The Bank claims it has never lost contractual principal or interest except in two still-outstanding cases — **Zimbabwe** (in arrears since October 2000; owed about $1.5 billion at last count — a quarter-century zombie debt kept technically alive by token payments of about $50 million a year since 2023) and **Belarus** (in arrears since 2022). The economics of default: average loss-given-default under 10 percent (a high of 58.5 percent for a twenty-year nonaccrual), because the real punishment is exclusion and the eventual arrears-clearance settlement — a donor lends the country the money to pay the Bank back, the Bank immediately lends the country a policy operation big enough to repay the bridge, and everyone restarts the clock. Sudan's 2021 sequence is the template: a US bridge loan ($1.15 billion, January 2021, announced December 2020) repaid within weeks by a Bank policy loan of $1.365 billion (approved March 25–26, 2021 — the first new Bank lending to Sudan in three decades), the IMF clearing its share ($1.39 billion) in June with a French bridge, the African Development Bank ($413M) in May with a UK bridge plus Swedish and Irish contributions; then the HIPC decision point in June 2021 ($23 billion of relief promised against $56 billion of debt), and — within two years — war again in April 2023.

### 8.5 The Joint Debt-Sustainability Machine (with the IMF)

For poor countries, borrowing permission is computed jointly: the **LIC Debt Sustainability Framework** (April 2005; reviewed 2017 and again in 2024, the latter approved September 2026) classifies countries by debt-carrying capacity (a composite of CPIA, growth, reserves, remittances, and world growth) and applies thresholds per indicator: weak capacity — external debt present-value at 30 percent of GDP, 140 percent of exports, debt service at 10 percent of exports and 14 percent of revenue, public debt PV at 35 percent of GDP; medium capacity — 40/180/15/18/55; strong — 55/240/21/23/70. Risk ratings (low, moderate, high, in distress) drive IDA's grant-versus-loan decision and the application of the Sustainable Development Finance Policy. Division of labor: the IMF leads the three-to-five-year macroeconomic projections; the Bank leads the long-term growth-investment analysis; disagreements have a formal escalation path. For middle-income countries there is a separate framework; and for actually-distressed sovereigns the G20's **Common Framework** (November 2020, Riyadh) organizes official creditors (a Paris Club co-chair plus a non-Paris-club co-chair — China in the early cases) with the Bank and IMF supplying the debt-sustainability analysis and a procedural requirement that nettles private creditors: multilateral development banks must keep net flows positive as a condition of anyone else's relief — so the Bank lends into distress before private creditors share pain, a rule private creditors know and exploit. Cases: Chad (agreement November 2022 — China's debut as co-chair, France the other, Glencore a commercial participant), Zambia (official-creditor deal October 2023; bondholder exchange June 2024 — about $840 million of relief foregone; the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and China Development Bank signed in September 2024; state-contingent clauses assessed June 2026), Ghana, Ethiopia; middle-income Sri Lanka (2022–24) ran outside the Framework, leaning on an Indian bridge (~$4 billion, exempted from restructuring), a Bank emergency package (about $600 million in 2022, plus a later $712 million of loan conversions from floating to fixed rate that saved some $38 million in present value), and an IMF program — while $900 million in multilateral payments were kept current. The Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable (2023) maintains the negotiation table.

### 8.5A The Environmental and Social Framework

The rules that bind every project — and the battleground of the Bank's legitimacy — are the **Environmental and Social Framework (ESF)**: approved August 4, 2016, effective October 1, 2018 for all new investment projects, replacing eight decades-old safeguard policies (the Operational Policies on environmental assessment, natural habitats, forests, pest management, cultural property, involuntary resettlement, Indigenous peoples, and safety of dams). Its architecture: a **Vision** statement; an **Environmental and Social Policy (ESP)** — the Bank's own obligations, including a four-tier risk classification (High, Substantial, Moderate, Low), risk-based due diligence, the incorporation of an Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP) into the legal agreement, disclosure and consultation requirements, monitoring, and a grievance redress mechanism; **ten Environmental and Social Standards** that the *borrower* must meet — (1) Assessment and Management of Environmental and Social Risks; (2) Labor and Working Conditions; (3) Resource Efficiency and Pollution Prevention; (4) Community Health and Safety; (5) Land Acquisition, Restrictions on Land Use, and Involuntary Resettlement; (6) Biodiversity Conservation; (7) Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved Traditional Local Communities; (8) Cultural Heritage; (9) Financial Intermediaries; (10) Stakeholder Engagement and Information Disclosure; plus a Directive on addressing risks and impacts on disadvantaged or vulnerable individuals or groups, and the general Environmental, Health and Safety Guidelines defining good international industry practice. The borrower's process runs: environmental and social assessment per ESS1; an ESCP translating findings into contract language; an Environmental and Social Management System, Management Plan, or Resettlement Action Plan as the risk requires; a stakeholder engagement plan; a functioning grievance mechanism; monitoring and reporting in ISR cycles; and the mitigation hierarchy (avoid, minimize, mitigate, compensate). Financial-intermediary projects route through the client's own ESMS with risk-based prior review of high-risk subprojects. The framework was forged in four consultation phases (2012–14) reaching roughly eight thousand stakeholders in sixty-three countries, ran a seven-year parallel operation with the old policies, and faces its first full developmental evaluation in FY2026 — while the 2026 restructuring cuts the safeguards staffing that operate it (Part VII, §35).

### 8.5B The IFC Investment Cycle: Twelve Gates

The IFC runs a private-equity-grade process, twelve stages long: **(1) Business Development** — investment officers originate; of roughly one hundred concepts screened, about twenty reach concept review; **(2) Early Review** — a weekly Concept Review Meeting of country manager and Washington specialists, twenty minutes per project, about half advance; **(3) PDSER approval** — an eight-to-twelve-page Project Data Sheet Early Review goes to the Corporate Investment Committee (the vice presidents and chief executive, for controversial deals) or to regional delegated authority (industry director, regional manager, chief credit officer); **(4) Appraisal and Due Diligence** — site visits, financial and economic soundness, Performance Standards compliance, and scoring on the Anticipated Impact Measurement and Monitoring (AIMM) system; **(5) the Investment Review Meeting** — at headquarters, the industry director chairing, investment team plus country and industry managers, credit officers, legal, and environmental-social advisors; roughly three hours; about four in five pass; term sheets finalized; **(6) Public Disclosure** — the Summary of Investment Information and Environmental and Social Review Summary posted on the website, with the disclosure period varying by category; **(7) Board Review and Approval** — streamlined circulation for small low-risk deals, formal sessions for large or controversial ones (board members may force discussion of any item); **(8) Commitment** — legal agreements and covenants; **(9) Disbursement** — staged and conditional; **(10) Supervision and Development Outcome Tracking** — the Development Outcome Tracking System (DOTS) scored quarterly, portfolio reviews by an Operations Committee; **(11) Evaluation** — an annual random sample, with the Independent Evaluation Group auditing independently; **(12) Closing.** Discipline runs alongside: credit ratings assigned at defined stages by an independent risk function, prudential limits (single-client, country-exposure, sector-concentration caps) approved by the Corporate Risk Committee, a Portfolio Committee (since FY18) for valuations, and a cycle time compressed from 240 to 140 days under delegation. The mobilization machinery around it: the **MCPP** (Managed Co-Lending Portfolio Program — $10 billion-plus pledged by eighteen global institutional investors, 2017 onward, automated participation in IFC loans) and the equity-fund complex (the Asset Management Company created 2009, merged into IFC in January 2020; its sixteen funds have raised over $10 billion from sixty-plus investors, with IFC ownership stakes ranging from roughly 13 to 61 percent across the Africa, Latin America, Catalyst, infrastructure, financial-institutions-growth, and women-entrepreneurs funds) — the vehicle structure through which most of IFC's third-party money, and the pattern-five controversies of Part V, flow.

### 8.5C The MIGA Underwriting Process

MIGA's guarantee production line: **(1) Preliminary Application** — free, confidential, online; registers "new investment" eligibility and issues a Notice of Registration (no commitment); **(2) Definitive Application** — fees of $5,000 (projects to $25 million) or $10,000 above, credited against the first premium, plus a $25,000 processing fee for specialized due diligence; **(3) initial screening and kick-off** — assignment of an underwriter; **(4) underwriting** — development-impact analysis, environmental and social review (with the Exclusion List barring weapons, tobacco, gambling, and similar from coverage), integrity checks against the sanctions lists, economic and financial viability, pricing and reinsurance placement (about 68 percent of the book is reinsured); **(5) the Second Internal Review Meeting** — for Small Investment Program guarantees, approval sits with the program's director (no board); larger projects go to **(6) disclosure** — the Summary of Proposed Guarantee and ESRS published, with host-country government approval required (30-day no-objection windows; 60 days for Category A projects, 30 for B, 15 for C, 15 for small-investment guarantees); **(7) Board approval** — streamlined for guarantees at or below $25 million (the President's Report circulates twelve business days; any executive director may call a formal discussion) or formal above it; **(8) contract execution** — after internal approvals, board approval, host-country consent, and disclosure expiry; **(9) monitoring, contract maintenance, and claims** — when covered events occur (expropriation, war and civil disturbance, currency inconvertibility, breach of contract), claims are assessed against the contract; Ukraine's war-risk claims under the 2023–26 frameworks are the first of their kind in the agency's history. MIGA's own crisis instrument — the COVID-19 Response Program (April 2020) — issued $7.6 billion across 47 projects within two years.

### 8.5D The IMF–World Bank Concordat: The Divisions of Labor

How the twin institutions divide the world is governed by a largely invisible document: the **1989 Concordat** (formally, the "Framework for Collaboration"), negotiated after a decade of turf war in which both institutions ran structural-adjustment programs in the same countries with overlapping conditionality. Its origin is itself instructive: Conable's March 10, 1989 memo resisted an early US draft's "primary responsibility" language, and negotiators joked about "vertical slicing of the baby"; the final text replaced the 1966 understanding with a clean division. The Fund is primary in: surveillance (Article IV consultations), exchange-rate matters, balance-of-payments adjustment, and the "aggregate aspects" of macroeconomic policy (aggregate public spending and revenues, aggregate wage and price policies, money and credit aggregates, interest and exchange rates). The Bank is primary in: development strategies and programs, sector and project investment, structural adjustment, the composition and efficiency of public expenditure, resource allocation between the public and private sectors, administrative-system reform, production-trade-and-financial-sector reforms, public-enterprise restructuring, and sector policies. Rules of engagement in shared territory: the non-primary institution defers to the primary institution's judgment; both maintain regular senior-staff meetings, procedures to resolve differences, systematic information exchange, and joint ad hoc study groups. The follow-on machinery: the **Malan Report** (February 2007 — the External Review Committee on IMF–World Bank Collaboration, finding persistent overlap and competition), the **Joint Management Action Plan** (2008 — mandating annual country-team meetings, monitored by both institutions' strategy units), and a Guidance Note on information sharing under which Bank staff attend IMF program meetings and comment on program documents without engagement rights. The Greece-era revelation (Part V §24) — that the real tie-breaking forum in a crisis is the Group of Seven (G7) — is the Concordat's unwritten amendment.

### 8.5E The Access to Information Regime

The Bank's transparency rules are themselves a product of scandal cycles: the original 1994 disclosure policy followed the Narmada-era pressure; the current **Access to Information Policy (2010)** makes proactive disclosure the default — project documents, Board calendars, country partnership frameworks, procurement awards, and research all publish automatically. The exceptions: personal information; executive directors' office communications; Ethics Committee material; attorney-client privileged advice; security-sensitive information; information provided by a member in confidence; certain corporate-administrative material; and banking information whose disclosure would prejudice the Bank or its clients. The declassification ladder: "Official Use Only" material opens after five years, "Confidential" after ten, "Strictly Confidential" after twenty; board minutes post after the deliberation concludes; archival declassification runs eight to sixteen weeks. The regime's 2026 test case is the GRAD trustee agreement (Part VII, §34) — an agreement the Bank declines to publish, on an instrument whose money is already moving.

## 9. The Legal Fortress

The Bank's immunities are not courtesy; they are constitutional. **Article VII** of the IBRD Articles (Article VIII for IDA; the IFC, MIGA, and ICSID conventions carry equivalents): §3 — immunity from legal process except when the Bank itself sues or agrees to arbitrate, and a bar on suits *by member countries or persons deriving claims from them* (a borrower cannot sue the Bank that lent to it); §4 — property and assets immune from seizure, expropriation, or requisition by executive or legislative action, before final judgment; §5 — archives inviolable; §6 — assets free from restrictions, controls, and moratoria of every kind; §7 — communications privileges; §8 — officials and executive directors immune from legal process for official acts (waivable only by the Bank), plus immigration and exchange facilities; §9 — total tax immunity — no taxation of the Bank, its assets, its securities, or the salaries of non-locally-hired staff; §10 — members must implement these immunities in domestic law. US law does so through the International Organizations Immunities Act of 1945.

Two consequences follow, and both are documented in case law. First, no borrower, employee, or victim can sue the World Bank in any court for anything it does as a Bank. US courts dismissed Karen Hudes's whistleblower case on precisely this ground; *Jam v. International Finance Corporation* (Indian fishing communities harmed by a coal plant the IFC financed) reached the US Supreme Court, which in February 2019 held the presumption of international-organization immunity nearly absolute (the IFC had partially waived immunity in its Articles for arbitration, but the Court read even commercial-activity exceptions narrowly) — the parties ultimately settled. Canada's Supreme Court went further in *World Bank Group v. Wallace* (April 29, 2016): the integrity records of the Bank's investigators are covered by archive immunity, which is **not waivable** — even a criminal investigation (the Padma Bridge case, where the Royal Canadian Mounted Police had wiretaps) could not compel production; the intervenors on the Bank's side included Transparency International, the OECD, and four sister development banks; on the other side, civil-liberties groups argued immunity should be functional, not absolute. Second, the only justice available inside the walls is the justice the Bank grants: the **World Bank Administrative Tribunal** (created 1980; seven judges; answer due in 60 days, reply 45, rejoinder 30; ~8 months per case; awards final and binding), whose record matters: prevailing whistleblowers are reinstated in fewer than 15 percent of cases, and its 2018 Decision No. 437 — finding a (Ugandan) staff lawyer's termination "arbitrary" and an "abuse of discretion" while dismissing his retaliation claim in a single sentence and his racial-discrimination claims without analysis — became the emblem of internal accountability (the Government Accountability Project, which represented him, published a parallel study documenting systemic racial discrimination in outcomes).

## 10. The Accountability Machinery Inside

- **INT (Integrity Vice Presidency)** — created as the Department of Institutional Integrity in April 2001 after the consultant-trust-fund scandal; reformed by the Thornburgh review (2002) and the Volcker Independent Panel (2007). It receives complaints (over 1,400 a year in recent annual reports), triages (roughly a third to nine-tenths "not fully assessed" or closed without investigation), runs preliminary inquiries and full investigations, issues Final Investigation Reports, and refers completed cases to the sanctions process and to national authorities (FY23 referrals went to authorities in Germany, the DRC, the European Union's anti-fraud office (OLAF), Tajikistan, Pakistan, Kenya, and Egypt). FY2025: 65 new external investigations; 19 sanction cases and 19 settlements submitted.
- **The sanctions system** — two tiers since 2007 (the 1996–2001 era had a single sanctions committee): a first-tier officer — the Chief Suspension and Debarment Officer for IBRD/IDA; Evaluation Officers for IFC/MIGA/guarantees — reviews INT's Statement of Accusations and Evidence, generally issues a Show Cause Letter first, imposes temporary suspension upon a Notice of Sanctions Proceedings (or earlier where evidence supports a two-year-plus sanction), allows 90 days to contest, and issues uncontested determinations that become final and are published. Mitigation can reduce sanctions (minor role up to 25 percent; voluntary corrective action up to 50 percent; cooperation up to a third). The **Sanctions Board** — seven *external* judges (the FY2025–26 roster: Philip Daltrop as chair, Nassib Ziadé — who also presides over the IMF and UN administrative tribunals — Claudia Annacker, Mahnaz Malik, Adedoyin Rhodes-Vivour, Don Scott De Amicis, Khaled Koubaa), non-renewable six-year terms — hears appeals and issues final determinations. Sanctions: debarment (with or without conditional release — the most common outcome at roughly 48 percent of sanctioned respondents), conditional non-debarment, letters of reprimand; settlements are reviewed for voluntariness and guideline compliance. Since April 9, 2010, a **cross-debarment agreement** means any major development bank's debarment (World Bank, African Development Bank, Asian Development Bank, European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, Inter-American Development Bank; the AIIB joined later) is honored by all — a firm expelled once is expelled everywhere (the AIIB cross-debarred a Moscow rail-signals company in August 2026 that the Bank had debarred through 2032). Roughly 1,250 entities from around 120 countries have been debarred since 1996; the debarred list is public and checked at every procurement evaluation; successor entities can inherit sanctions ("successor doctrine" — used against CCCC's affiliates, Part V). FY2025 outcomes: 19 sanctioned through uncontested determinations, 67 percent carrying integrity conditions, average processing 11.1 months.
- **Staff misconduct** — separate track: Staff Rule 8.01 investigations; discipline from oral reprimand to termination with restitution; appeal to the Administrative Tribunal. FY2023: five staff investigations, three substantiated, one termination.
- **The Inspection Panel** — created by board resolution in September 1993 (Resolution 93-10) after the Narmada dam revolt: the first independent accountability mechanism in a multilateral institution. Three members appointed by the board for non-renewable five-year terms (the lineage of members includes Ramanie Kunanayagam — later chair of the Asian Development Bank's compliance panel — Imrana Jalal of Fiji as chair, Mark Goldsmith, and, from the reform era, Evelyn Dietsche). People harmed by a Bank project can request an inspection alleging the Bank violated its own policies. The phases (as reformed by the 2025 Accountability Mechanism resolution, merging compliance review with a Dispute Resolution Service): receipt and registration (15 business days; criteria — non-frivolous, project supported by the Bank, plausible link to harm, timing within 15 months of closing for post-2020 projects); management response (21 business days); the panel's eligibility and recommendation (21 more days; a field visit; plausible causal link; serious character); board decision (absence of objection; a director can force discussion, but the board does not judge merits); an optional dispute-resolution window (30 days to decide, up to a year plus six months; the panel's compliance work pauses); investigation (a lead inspector; public investigation plan; roughly six months; the draft shared with requesters); management report and action plan (six weeks); board approval; public disclosure within two weeks; and panel verification of the action plan's implementation, with annual reporting. The original 1993 design gave the panel twenty-one-day cycles and left eligibility interpretation with the executive directors; the 2025 reform shifted interpretation to the panel itself.
- **IEG (Independent Evaluation Group)** — the successor to McNamara's Operations Evaluation Department (1973) and the later independent evaluation offices of IFC and MIGA (merged 2006): evaluates the institution's own work and reports to the board through CODE. Directors-general have included Caroline Heider, Vinod Thomas, and Alison Evans (2019–2023; her successor's appointment was not publicly announced as of this writing). Budget roughly $44–47 million a year — against the executive directors' offices' $85 million.
- **The Auditor-General and external audit** — the internal audit function reports to the Audit Committee of the board; external financial statements are audited by a private firm on a rotation regime (a maximum ten-year tenure; audit firms are barred from selling consulting to the institution; KPMG has been the long-standing incumbent; the US GAO audits the oversight arrangements themselves periodically).

## 11. The Scoreboard and the Data Empire

The current **Corporate Scorecard** (FY2024–2030) compresses the institution's ambitions into 22 indicators, down from roughly 150: vision indicators (extreme-poverty headcount — the line was $2.15 a day from September 2022 until June 2025, when a revision of purchasing-power-parity data raised it to **$3.00 a day**, adding about 125 million people to the counted extreme poor and bringing the 2024 global estimate to about 817 million; plus a "prosperity gap" against a $25-a-day benchmark and inequality measures); client-context indicators tracking SDG progress; and Bank results indicators (safety-net coverage, learning poverty, stunting, essential-health-service access, emissions, electricity access, water, food security, transport, broadband, gender finance, jobs, private capital mobilized). A mid-term review is due in 2026.

The Bank's flagship statistical products make it the world's default source of development data: the **World Development Indicators**; the annual **World Development Report** (since 1978 — the catalogue runs from 1980's *Poverty and Human Development* through 1990's *Poverty*, the 1993 *The East Asian Miracle*, the 2000/01 *Attacking Poverty* — during whose drafting Ravi Kanbur resigned in protest at pressure to soften its equity findings — 2006's *Equity and Development*, 2019's *The Changing Nature of Work* (the anchor of the Human Capital Index), 2021's *Data for Better Lives*, and 2024's *The Middle-Income Trap*; each produced over roughly a year by a research team with an academic advisory committee and a high-level advisory panel); the **Global Findex** financial-inclusion database; **Women, Business and the Law**; the **Poverty and Inequality Platform** (the statistical engine: household surveys for more than 160 countries, benchmarked to the International Comparison Program's purchasing-power-parity rounds); and **B-READY** (Business Ready), the successor to Doing Business — first published in October 2024 for 50 economies, expanding to 101 economies in the 2025 edition and to full coverage of 160-plus economies in 2026, built on three pillars (regulatory framework, public services, operational efficiency) with a Decision Review Meeting process for each economy's data (two weeks' embargo, two days for comments, three to five official peer reviewers) and — deliberately, after Doing Business — no headline ranking.

# PART III — THE PRESIDENTS

Thirteen men have run the World Bank, plus one acting chief. The office selects for a type — eight of the thirteen came directly from finance or industry — and each presidency maps onto an era of development thinking. Dates below are service dates.

## 12. The Founders and Builders (1946–1968)

**Eugene Meyer (June–December 1946).** Six months; a Wall Street fortune behind him (copper, mining, the Federal Reserve chairmanship, the Washington Post), he staffed the new institution's core (Smith, McLain, Pineo, Rist, Demuth, Broches) and set its first standard — no loan before the machine could carry it. His clash with US executive director Emilio Collado over who governs ended with his resignation; the question stayed open until McCloy answered it.

**John J. McCloy (1947–1949).** The ultimatum that made the presidency real ("we would go on back to New York that afternoon"); the first bond issues (including the first non-dollar borrowing, Swiss francs at 2.5 percent, in 1947); the France, Netherlands, Denmark, Chile, and Brazil loans; a hand in the Marshall Plan's creation; and the United Nations relationship agreement. Two years, and the institution's spine.

**Eugene Black (1949–1963).** Thirteen and a half years — the institutionalizer. "Black's Bank" became a Wall Street commonplace; the 1959 capital doubling was secured on his watch; IFC was born in 1956; and Aswan taught the lesson. Black's operating style — cultivate the US Treasury, court the bond market, never surprise the board — became the professional template every successor has followed or paid for ignoring.

**George Woods (1963–1968).** The First Boston investment banker who presided over the pivot from reconstruction to development. Woods pushed lending toward agriculture and education, doubled down on infrastructure, and set up the Pearson Commission (the Commission on International Development, 1969) that would define the aid debates of the next decade. His presidency also saw the first serious cracks with the borrowers: the UNCTAD era was dawning, and the developing world was beginning to organize as a bloc.

## 13. Robert McNamara (1968–1981): The Empire of Expertise

Robert McNamara arrived from the Pentagon and Ford Motor Company and transformed the Bank more than anyone before or since. He borrowed the defense model — systems analysis, quantified objectives, program budgeting — and pointed it at poverty.

The numbers of his era: lending roughly doubled in his first two years and kept climbing (from about $1 billion a year on arrival toward $12 billion-plus commitments by departure); the IDA replenishments tripled in size (IDA5 in 1977 was $7.7 billion); cofinancing arrangements multiplied; and the Bank's membership expanded as decolonization filled the map with new borrowers. His 1973 Nairobi Annual Meetings speech — "absolute poverty... degrading and inhuman" — made poverty reduction the institution's explicit mission in a way it had never been on paper. The World Development Report was born (the 1980 inaugural issue on poverty and human development was his capstone). He launched the Onchocerciasis Control Programme in West Africa — river blindness — which protected tens of millions from a blinding parasite; it remains the institution's single most celebrated health achievement and the proof-of-concept for everything from GAVI to the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR).

He also built the research empire (the Development Research Department, hiring the best development economists money could buy) and the population agenda — family planning lending, controversial at the time with the Catholic countries and later anathema to American conservatives. And he built the borrowing machine: in the 1970s the oil states' surpluses needed parking and the developing world needed dollars; the Bank borrowed the petrodollars from capital markets and intermediated them outward, mostly to Latin America's state enterprises. When interest rates turned in 1979–82, that intermediation became the debt crisis.

The famous 1991 Wapenhans report — the "Portfolio Management Task Force," written after his departure — examined the McNamara portfolio and found the share of projects in major trouble had doubled, from about 11 percent (FY1981) to 20 percent (FY1991) of ongoing projects (the 37.5 percent "unsatisfactory" figure covered a broader sample), and diagnosed an "approval culture": pressure to lend rewarded volume and speed, and supervision was the first casualty. The environmental reckoning arrived simultaneously: the Bank's own Operations Evaluation Department rated Polonoroeste (the Brazilian Amazon highway, begun 1981) a failure, and the Narmada project in India was spiraling toward the confrontation of Part V. McNamara left in June 1981 as the debt crisis began. His epitaph inside the institution is genuinely two-sided: the man who made the Bank a development institution, and the man whose volume machine created the conditions for the lost decade.

## 14. The Adjustment Years (1981–1995)

**A.W. Clausen (1981–1986).** The Bank of America chairman steered the institution into its structural-adjustment era: policy-based lending (quick-disbursing loans against economic reforms) exploded from a fifth of commitments toward half; the first structural adjustment loans went to Kenya and Mauritius; the private-sector and market-economy agenda hardened; and the McNamara-era poverty emphasis was, in the words of critics, "depoliticized" into growth-first economics. Clausen's appointment of Stanley Fischer as chief economist (1988, arriving as Clausen left) and Anne Krueger as his successor set the intellectual tone. Clausen also presided over the deepest staff cuts in the institution's history (the 1987–88 retrenchment began under him) and the Bank's most controversial mining and energy portfolio. His five years saw the debt crisis at its worst — the 1985 Baker Plan (Treasury Secretary James Baker's proposal: $29 billion of new money over three years for fifteen major debtors in exchange for market reforms) and the 1987 Brady Plan's beginnings.

**Barber Conable (1986–1991).** A former congressman from New York, Conable was recruited by Baker himself. His legacy is threefold. First, the **1987 reorganization**: collapsing the Bank's regional and project hierarchies into a flatter structure with country departments and sectoral vice presidencies — the staff called it "musical chairs," and a wave of senior departures followed. Second, the environmental reckoning: Polonoroeste's exposure led to the Bank's first environmental strategy and the creation of the **Global Environment Facility** (1991) as an experiment in global environmental funding — piloted inside the Bank, then made permanent. Third, the scandals at the edges: the BCCI collapse (1991) revealed that the Bank, despite everything, had kept correspondent relationships with the fraudulent bank; Conable's own integrity review found lapses. He accepted the creation of the World Development Report as an annual, and commissioned the review of portfolio quality that became Wapenhans.

**Lew Preston (1991–1995).** The J.P. Morgan chairman inherited the storm. June 18, 1992: the Independent Review of India's Sardar Sarovar (Narmada) projects — the Morse Commission — reported that the Bank was in breach of its own resettlement and environmental policies on essentially every count (Part V). 1992: the Wapenhans report quantified the approval culture. December 1991: an internal memo by chief economist Larry Summers's office — arguing, satirically or not, that pollution should be traded toward "the countries where the lowest wages" — leaked to the press and became the symbol of adjustment-era arrogance. Preston's responses built the modern quality apparatus: a portfolio-management reform, the creation of the Inspection Panel (1993 — the first independent accountability mechanism in any multilateral institution, accepted over the fierce internal resistance of the operations side), and the Quality Assurance Group. He also opened the Bank to the ex-socialist world: the first Russia loans, the CIS trust funds, and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development's design. He died in office in May 1995, of cancer.

**James Wolfensohn (1995–2005).** The Australian-born investment banker, cellist, and Olympic fencer served a full decade and fought for nearly every day of it. His signature battles:

- **The corruption war (1996).** His "cancer of corruption" speech to the board — declaring that the Bank's own staff estimated that a significant share of loan money was misused — nearly split the institution. Borrowers heard neo-colonialism; staff heard a repudiation of their borrowers; the US Congress heard a reason to fund. The campaign produced the first anti-corruption strategy, the Institutional Integrity Department's strengthening, and a decade of governance lending. It also collided with Wolfensohn's diplomacy: when the campaign threatened relations with big borrowers (and, reportedly, with the Clinton administration's China policy), it was quietly de-emphasized — the first full cycle of Pattern Six (reform amnesia).
- **The Comprehensive Development Framework (1999)** and *Voices of the Poor* (2000) — the "country ownership" era: strategies written with governments, and a landmark study that actually asked poor people what poverty means (the answer: insecurity, humiliation, dependency — not just low income).
- **The East Asian crisis (1997–99)** — the Bank's biggest analytical failure: it missed the crash entirely, then produced, with the IMF, the largest rescue packages in history for Thailand, Indonesia, and Korea; chief economist Joe Stiglitz broke ranks publicly over the IMF's contractionary medicine and resigned in November 1999.
- **HIPC (1996)** — the Highly Indebted Poor Countries initiative, born on his watch, and the first genuine multilateral debt-relief mechanism.
- **The Strategic Compact (1997)** — a $250-million internal reorganization toward the "knowledge bank," with staff quality, decentralization, and information technology at its core; and the **fiftieth anniversary** (1994), which he used to force a public reckoning with the institution's history.

He resigned (at the second attempt — he had offered in 1999 and been persuaded to stay) in May 2005. His decade is the hinge of the modern Bank: everything after him — the governance agenda, the knowledge identity, the poverty mission's softening and hardening — is a response to something he started.

**Paul Wolfowitz (2005–2007).** The Bush administration sent the architect of the Iraq War to the Bank; the marriage lasted twenty-three months. His substantive agenda was real: an Africa Action Plan with doubled commitments; a hard-line anti-corruption department (the Institutional Integrity VP under Suzanne Rich Folsom) that actually debarred ministers' favorite contractors; governance conditionality pushed into country strategies. His political method destroyed him. The anatomy, in documented detail: shortly after arriving he arranged the transfer of his companion, Shaha Riza (a Bank communications officer in the Middle East department), out of the institution to the US State Department — with a raise of $47,300 to $180,000 in 2005 against a 12 percent ceiling, and a further raise to $193,590 in 2006 against a 3.7 percent ceiling, plus promotion guarantees; an internal ethics committee had told him to recuse himself, and he did not. He staffed senior ranks with political allies on salaries around $250,000 (Kevin Kellems, Robin Cleveland). His budget director, Juan José Daboub, was caught ordering the deletion of family-planning language and reproductive-health references from Bank strategy documents — a scandal within the scandal. He publicly waterboarded the Bank's own climate language in a major strategy speech. The staff revolted (a formal no-confidence vote of the Staff Association); the board's ad hoc committee found he had breached his obligations; and on May 17, 2007, he announced his resignation effective June 30 — the only president in history driven out by scandal. Robert Zoellick was nominated May 30 and took office July 1. The episode's lesson: the institution can expel a president, but only when he defies both the board and the shareholders — and the reforms his expulsion supposedly heralded mostly evaporated.

**Robert Zoellick (2007–2012).** A US trade representative, deputy secretary of state, and Goldman Sachs vice chairman who restored calm and then rebuilt. His October 2007 "six strategic themes" set the modern agenda (the poorest countries, fragile states, the Arab world, middle-income countries, global public goods, and the knowledge agenda). The crisis made him: the 2008–10 surge committed $100 billion (July 2008–April 2010) — crisis loans to Latvia, Hungary, and Mexico within weeks — and the Vulnerability Financing Facility and food-crisis programs followed. The **2010 capital increase** ($86.2 billion subscribed; $5.1 billion paid-in; the first "dynamic formula" for voting shares; a third African executive director added; the share of votes at the IMF-quota link broken modestly) was his architecture, with then-US Treasury's Lael Brainard negotiating the bargain. He opened the Bank's data vaults (the open-data initiative), championed the Accra–Busan aid-effectiveness agenda, and walked the portfolio back toward infrastructure. He left in 2012 having doubled lending capacity; his private lament — that in 2008 the world saved its banks in weeks and its poor in years — frames the era's moral accounting.

**Jim Kim (2012–2019).** The physician-anthropologist, former WHO HIV/AIDS director, and Dartmouth president arrived with a Silicon-Valley-flavored promise to make the Bank a "solutions bank" with measurable deliverables. His record: the **twin goals** (end extreme poverty by 2030; boost shared prosperity for the bottom 40 percent in every country); the **Human Capital Project** and its Index (launched at Bali in 2018; a country's survival-school-health composite, methodology led by Aart Kraay, anchored to the 2019 WDR); the largest-ever capital package (2018: $60 billion endorsed at the Spring Meetings — $7.5 billion IBRD paid-in, $5.5 billion IFC paid-in, $52.6 billion callable — with conditions on below-GDI (gross domestic income) lending focus); the 2014 Global Practices reorganization (fourteen Practices over five cross-cutting solution areas, overlaying the country matrix) which staff surveys savaged (34 percent said senior management maintained positive morale; the Staff Association publicly revolted; a compensation bonus scandal — a $94,000 retention bonus to a departing managing director, widely leaked — crystallized the anger); and the COVID-era acceleration of the IDA Private Sector Window. In January 2019, with three years left on his term, he resigned abruptly to join a private infrastructure investment firm (later folded into BlackRock's Global Infrastructure Partners). Kristalina Georgieva — his chief executive officer — served as acting president for two months before David Malpass took office. The abruptness was itself a scandal: he had, months earlier, refused to answer board questions about his outside board roles; the institution he left was mid-reorganization, morale-broken, and facing the Doing Business time bomb already ticking inside it.

**David Malpass (2019–2023).** A Trump Treasury official, former Bear Stearns chief economist, and longtime Bank skeptic — he had called the institution's lending to China "ridiculous" — Malpass was confirmed over objections that he was the first appointee openly hostile to the institution's climate role. His presidency produced the largest operational mobilization in the Bank's history (COVID: $157 billion in fifteen months, $160 billion in the fifteen-month envelope, $204 billion in the first two calendar years; vaccine financing scaled from a $12 billion pledge to $20 billion), the 2021 Climate Change Action Plan (35 percent average climate share, rising to 45 percent later), and the Doing Business scandal's eruption on his watch (September 2021: the WilmerHale report; he discontinued the report the same day). His own self-inflicted wound came on September 20, 2022, at a New York climate-event panel, when asked whether he accepted that fossil-fuel burning was warming the planet, he answered: "I'm not a scientist." The European climate ministers called for his resignation; the White House noted its disagreement; and on February 15, 2023 — after a Zelensky meeting, a Ukraine portfolio surge, and months of speculation — he announced his resignation effective June 2023.

**Ajay Banga (2023– ).** MasterCard's former chief executive (2010–2020), a vice chairman at General Atlantic, and the first Sikh-American to lead the institution, nominated by the Biden administration in February 2023, confirmed unopposed May 3, taking office June 2, 2023. His mandate was reform, and his first two years produced real change: the **Evolution Roadmap** (endorsed October 2023) rewrote the mission — "ending poverty on a livable planet" — created the new scorecard, launched the job-creation strategic theme (the 2025 Development Committee communiqué carried "Jobs: The Path to Prosperity"), cut preparation times from nineteen to twelve months, drove through the IDA21 architecture ($100 billion envelope; the GROW window financing global public goods at $15.9 billion; a Crisis Response Window of $3.7 billion; total policy commitments cut to twenty-five), and scaled the guarantee platform. His second phase ran into shareholders: the 2025 shareholding review's "modest measures," the April 2026 Spring Meetings rupture, and then the Gaza instrument — his personal seat on the Board of Peace and the Bank's limited-trustee role in GRAD (Part VII). His five-year term runs to June 8, 2028; the succession debate he has provoked — including the argument that the next president should be neither American nor male — is itself now part of the institution's story.

## 15. The Balance of a Year, and the Next

**Fiscal 2024** (July 2023–June 2024): IBRD net commitments $37.6 billion; IDA $31.2 billion; IFC $21.5 billion of its own account plus $22.5 billion mobilized; MIGA $8.2 billion (a record); the World Bank Group total $117.5 billion.

**Fiscal 2025** (July 2024–June 2025) was the record: IBRD $40.9 billion across 139 operations ($17.5 billion investment project financing, $16.6 billion development policy financing, $6.8 billion program-for-results); IDA $39.9 billion across 303 operations; IFC $71.7 billion total financing (its own long-term finance $18.2 billion, mobilization $38.1 billion, short-term finance $15.4 billion — with India its largest exposure at $10.3 billion, then Brazil $7.1 billion, Türkiye $5.6 billion, South Africa $4.0 billion, China $3.1 billion); MIGA $9.5 billion in new guarantees across 44 projects (gross portfolio $36.8 billion, net $11.2 billion, 68 percent reinsured, net income $168 million). Combined: **$161.9 billion of commitments**. Disbursements also set records (IBRD+IDA above $80 billion); private capital mobilized was reported around $69.9 billion. Geographically: Sub-Saharan Africa first ($34.0 billion), Latin America and the Caribbean ($24.9 billion), Eastern Europe and Central Asia ($25.1 billion), South Asia ($13.2 billion), East Asia and the Pacific ($12.2 billion), Middle East and North Africa ($8.5 billion). Top borrowers: Ukraine first by a wide margin (exceeding $17 billion of outstanding IBRD exposure, the large majority third-party-guaranteed), then Turkey, Indonesia, India, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kenya, Pakistan, and Tanzania.

**Fiscal 2026** (partial, as of this writing): IBRD net commitments $50.2 billion for the year (147 operations — up 23 percent), with third-quarter loans outstanding at $283.6 billion; IDA net commitments $31.1 billion. The momentum is real; so is the concentration.

The sector allocation tells the twenty-first-century story: public administration and governance ($8.7 billion), transport ($6.7 billion), energy ($6.6 billion), social protection ($4.6 billion), financial sector ($3.6 billion), agriculture ($2.9 billion), health ($1.9 billion), industry ($1.9 billion), water ($1.7 billion), education ($1.3 billion), digital development ($0.8 billion). Health and education — the sectors where poverty is fought most cheaply — remain the smallest.

IDA's regional ledger: Africa holds about 54 percent of IDA loans outstanding, South Asia 20 percent. The FY26 country allocation top ten: Ethiopia $3.87 billion, Nigeria $2.41 billion, Kenya $1.7 billion, Bangladesh $1.27 billion, Pakistan $1.27 billion, Uganda $1.23 billion, Côte d'Ivoire $1.16 billion, DRC $1.03 billion, Benin $970 million, Madagascar $860 million. In one recent year, net flows to developing countries from all multilateral sources hit an all-time high of about $36 billion, with Bangladesh, Kenya, Nigeria, the Philippines, and Ukraine the largest recipients.

## 16. Climate Finance: The Build-Up and the Retreat

The trajectory: $17.2 billion in 2020; $29.4 billion (40 percent of IBRD/IDA) in FY2023; **$42.6 billion in FY2024** (a record); roughly **$50.8 billion World Bank Group-wide in FY2025** ($22.5 billion IBRD, $16.7 billion IDA, $8.3 billion IFC, $3.5 billion MIGA) — 48–49 percent of IBRD/IDA commitments, against the 45 percent target announced in December 2023. The methodology matters: the MDB joint approach applies Rio-Marker-style coefficients (an irrigation project can count as 40 percent climate); the Bank calls its share "climate co-benefits." Oxfam's *Climate Finance Unchecked* (October 2024) argued the counting inflates results: most "climate finance" is loans, only about $8 of every $100 arrives as grants, and much of it is existing lending re-tagged.

Then the reversal. At the April 2026 Spring Meetings, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called the climate framework "distortionary" and demanded its "long-overdue expiration"; nineteen of the twenty-five executive directors had signed a counter-letter affirming the need for both plan and target — the United States, Russia, Saudi Arabia, and Kuwait declined to sign; Japan and India abstained. On June 29, 2026, the Bank announced it would retire the 45 percent climate target and the plan's 35 percent predecessor, while extending the Climate Change Action Plan itself indefinitely pending an independent evaluation ordered by the board. IDA's 45 percent commitment survives inside its replenishment agreements through 2028. The Bank's framing — "shift from inputs to outcomes" — was read by its critics (including former evaluation director Vinod Thomas at Brookings) as a euphemism for capitulation, and bolstered by the counter-argument: climate finance had doubled, the MDBs jointly reported a record $162.5 billion for 2025 (up 21 percent; adaptation $35 billion up 31 percent; LMICs $103 billion), and the Bank's own record $51 billion was about half the system total. The environmental NGOs' verdict (NRDC): "mostly a symbolic victory" for the US — "if they can cave to one shareholder, other shareholders can take their money elsewhere." The developing-country bloc (the G11+ group of developing countries led by Brazil) demanded the Bank "remain firmly client-driven."

## 17. Ukraine: The Bank at War

Since Russia's February 2022 invasion, the Bank has been Ukraine's single largest external-financing conduit: **over $42 billion mobilized** (pledges and commitments by April 2024), more than $30 billion disbursed. The instruments: the **PEACE** budget-support program (over $23 billion, overwhelmingly grants from a multi-donor trust fund whose donors run from the United States and Japan to Norway, the Baltic states, and Indonesia); investment lending for energy and health (the HEAL project delivered millions of vaccine doses and kept primary care running; REPOWER restored electricity to millions; HOPE supported hundreds of thousands of households; ARISE aided tens of thousands of farmers); MIGA war-risk guarantees (the first of their kind, enabled by a US guarantee umbrella, a dedicated trust fund, the DFC co-insurance agreement of June 2023, and the 2026 war-risk insurance framework); the **FORTIS** Financial Intermediary Fund (established December 2024) holding the G7's extraordinary-revenue acceleration pledge — secured against immobilized Russian asset proceeds — to finance $50 billion of lending, of which the United States transferred $20 billion on December 10, 2024 (the Fund's first grants board approved $16 billion on December 13; the PEACE grant agreement of $15 billion was signed December 30); and the damage-assessment series (RDNA5, February 2026, priced a decade of reconstruction at $588 billion — roughly triple Ukraine's 2025 GDP — with direct damage above $195 billion; the Ukrainian government's 2025 budget showed a $9.96 billion gap). Ukraine became the IBRD's largest exposure; the Bank's financial statements carry it with the largest guarantee overlay in its history. Canada layered in an additional $1.3 billion loan guarantee in 2026 and the UK up to £4.1 billion of fiscal support through Bank guarantees. The war also produced the Bank's firmest geopolitical line: all lending to Russia and Belarus stopped March 2022 (Belarus entered nonaccrual; Russia prepaid its loans and departed as a borrower). No equivalent line exists anywhere else — the Bank continues to lend to countries on every side of every other conflict.

## 18. Capital Adequacy and the G20 Push

The "triple the MDBs" agenda — the G20 Independent Experts Group's 2023 "Triple Agenda" report (Larry Summers and N.K. Singh co-chairing) arguing the system could lend three times as much, recommending an $80 billion annual headroom expansion and a $20 billion Global Challenges financing mechanism — produced a sequence of capital-adequacy moves without a single new dollar of paid-in capital from the big shareholders: the equity-to-loans policy floor cut from 20 percent to 19 percent (2024) and then 18 percent (2025–26) — the Bank's own estimate $70–100 billion of extra lending capacity over a decade; bilateral guarantee limits raised from $10 billion to $20 billion; the hybrid-capital instrument ($773 million subscribed by eight shareholders by October 2024, growing toward ~$1 billion notional); the first portfolio-guarantee platform ($1.1 billion with two shareholders); Enhanced Callable Capital (New Zealand's $50 million first-mover, January 2026); and a steady drumbeat of portfolio-management tweaks (single-borrower limits, concentration measures). The 2018 capital package ($60 billion endorsed: $7.5 billion IBRD paid-in, $5.5 billion IFC paid-in, $52.6 billion callable; 93 percent paid in by early 2026, the subscription extended to April 2026) took six years to collect. Kharas and Bhattacharya's Brookings blueprint ("A Trillion-Dollar Bank," April 2023) priced the more ambitious path: triple IBRD to $80–100 billion a year using its existing equity — while noting the uncomfortable fact that the Bank's private-capital mobilization in 2019 was just $2.6 billion, and that partial credit guarantees had been called once. Every lever described above was pulled without an Articles amendment — because an amendment requires 85 percent, and 15.9 percent is American.

The MDB system coordination around it: the Heads of MDBs group (now ten institutions plus IMF participation — the African Development Bank, Asian Development Bank, Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, Council of Europe Development Bank, European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, European Investment Bank, Inter-American Development Bank, Islamic Development Bank, New Development Bank, and the World Bank Group, collectively the multilateral development banks (MDBs); the chair rotating annually: IDB 2024, CEB 2025, ADB 2026) issued its first joint Viewpoint Note (April 2024) and a roadmap endorsed at the November 2024 G20 (fourteen of sixteen deliverables); the joint climate-finance reporting (EIB-coordinated) hit $162.5–163 billion for 2025; and the group convenes now on everything from procurement standards (a common framework) to critical minerals.

## 19. Shareholding: The Reform That Never Comes

The 2025 Shareholding Review concluded, in the diplomats' word, with "modest measures." The dynamic formula proposed (80 percent GDP weight — 60 percent at market exchange rates, 40 percent at purchasing-power parity — plus 20 percent IDA contribution history, with the Lima Principles as guardrails) would shift real power toward China and away from Europe; the review adopted it only as a reference point. The historical ledger: the 2008–2010 crisis-era realignment shifted about 5 percentage points of voting power to developing countries; the 2018 review added half a point; the 2020 review did nothing; the 2024–25 review produced "modest measures." The 2025 Lima Principles remain aspirational. Meanwhile the data of disenfranchisement accumulate: the African Group 1 constituency's share of IBRD votes declined from about 2 percent to 1.38 percent across the reviews. Brazil's 2026 double-majority proposal — majority of votes *and* majority of countries to elect the president — stands as the first structural challenge to the appointment privilege; its fate is undecided. An April 2025 development-committee communiqué endorsed the "modest measures" language and asked for another review later in the decade, which is how the institution says not yet.

# PART V — CONFLICTS AND CORRUPTION: THE CASEBOOK

The ledger of the institution's failures — and of the wrongs done in, around, and through it. Cases are grouped by type, each tagged with its evidence status (defined in the introduction). Nothing here is allegation presented as fact; where a case is disputed, the dispute is the story.

## 20. The Adjustment Era (1981–2000)

**Structural adjustment** — the Bank's (with the IMF) signature program of the 1980s — exchanged balance-of-payments loans for policy overhauls: currency devaluations, subsidy cuts, price and trade liberalization, privatization, civil-service shrinkage. The intellectual frame was the **Washington Consensus** (a term coined by economist John Williamson in 1989: fiscal discipline, redirection of public spending toward health and education, tax reform, interest-rate liberalization, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, openness to foreign investment, privatization, deregulation, secure property rights). The theory: get prices right and growth follows. The practice produced the largest program of economic engineering in history — and the largest wave of protest.

The chronology of the policy: the 1982 Mexican default detonated the Latin American debt crisis; in 1985 Treasury Secretary James Baker proposed the **Baker Plan** — $29 billion of new money over three years for fifteen major debtors in exchange for market reforms; in 1989 his successor Nicholas Brady converted the approach into **debt reduction** — the Brady bonds (Mexico first, 1990; some $160 billion of bank debt eventually restructured at discounts up to 35 percent). The Bank and IMF financed the adjustment underneath both plans. Through the 1980s roughly three-quarters of IMF members (73 of 152) carried Fund arrangements; the Bank's adjustment lending rose from a fifth of commitments toward half. Africa's version was the lost decade: per-capita income shrank while countries carried dozens of adjustment loans each — Easterly's devastating count has the average high-adjustment African country receiving twenty-four adjustment loans and ending the period with negative per-capita growth. A leaked Bank staff memo on Suharto's Indonesia estimated family-and-crony leakage at about 30 percent of project value; the Bank's own adjustment reports conceded the medicine often didn't reach the patient.

The social record, in numbers. Walton and Seddon's canonical catalog counts **146 major protest episodes between 1976 and 1992** — strikes, riots, and mass demonstrations against adjustment measures. The named roll: the Caracazo in Venezuela (February 1989; transport fares and price liberalization under an IMF standby; estimates of 250 or more killed in the repression); Morocco 1990 (a general strike answered with troops; dozens killed; a five-star hotel set alight); Zambia's Copperbelt food riots (1985–86) that broke the Kaunda government's IMF program; the Central African Republic (1981 — protests, then a military coup); Nigeria (1988–89, and the 1986 SAP riots that killed students in Kaduna); Jordan 1989; Bolivia 1981–87 (a military coup followed by rolling general strikes); Brazil 1983/86/89; Chile 1983–84; Ecuador 1982–87; Haiti 1984; South Korea 1987; Peru 1981–85; the Philippines 1983–87. Modern econometrics confirms the mechanism: Reinsberg and colleagues find IMF-program participation alone raises protest counts by 11 percent or more even holding the austerity constant — a separate "alienation effect" of foreign imposition itself, strongest under left-wing governments and first-time borrowers, with hardship scaling with the size of the fiscal squeeze. The distributional record: across Latin America in the 1980s–90s the wage share of national income fell roughly 8 percentage points (Chile −24.4, Ecuador −18.8, Mexico −10.6), and by 1999 living standards had fallen below 1970s levels across much of the region; the "reparations" literature now treats adjustment's damages as a standing debt.

The Bank's own reckoning came from inside. The **Wapenhans report** (1992, the Portfolio Management Task Force) found the share of projects in major trouble had doubled — about 11 percent of the ongoing portfolio in FY1981 to 20 percent in FY1991 (37.5 percent "unsatisfactory" on a broader retrospective sample) — and diagnosed an "approval culture": pressure to lend rewarded volume and speed; supervision was the first casualty ("the front page problem": project problems visible early but rated satisfactory anyway). The remedies (the Quality Assurance Group, portfolio-management reform) were real and later partially dismantled — the seed of Pattern Six. Chief economist Joseph Stiglitz (1997–1999) broke ranks publicly — his 1999 critique of the IMF's Asian-crisis medicine ("the [IMF's] programs were not designed to restore economic equilibrium... the IMF's cookie-cutter approach") cost him his job; he announced his resignation in November 1999, effective January 2000, after Wolfensohn publicly criticized him at the annual meetings.

The debt itself was finally addressed at the turn of the century: the **HIPC Initiative** (1996; first decision points from 2000) and the **Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative** (2005, the G8 group of major economies) together canceled over $100 billion of debt for three dozen countries. The catch, written into every framework: the Bank, IMF, and African Development Bank exempted *themselves* from restructuring (preferred-creditor status), leaving the institutions' own claims intact while everyone else's were cut — a carve-out that survived even Rwanda's post-genocide relief (below). The completion of the arc: Sudan's 2021 HIPC decision point ($23 billion of relief against $56 billion of debt; 163 percent of GDP at decision) arrived thirty years after its arrears began, and war returned within two years.

## 21. The Mega-Project Casebook

**Narmada/Sardar Sarovar (India, 1985–1993).** The Bank lent $450 million toward the largest river-valley development in India's history — thirty large dams and thousands of smaller ones. From 1988 the Medha Patkar-led Narmada Bachao Andolan (Save Narmada Movement) made it the global test case. The Bank appointed an Independent Review — the Morse Commission (Bradford Morse, a former US congressman and UNDP administrator, with Thomas Berger, a Canadian judge) — which reported on June 18, 1992: the Bank in breach of its own resettlement and environmental policies on essentially every count; about 100,000 people displaced by submergence (the Commission put the canal-affected at 140,000 more); no land-for-land resettlement; costs "beyond any acceptable level." In March 1993 the Indian government asked the Bank to withdraw; the Bank cancelled the remainder. The dam complex was finished with Indian funds (the Sardar Sarovar's height was raised to its full 138.68 meters by 2017; a 2023 Supreme Court proceeding pressed completion of rehabilitation decades on). The institutional legacy: the Inspection Panel (1993) was the direct fruit; and the question the Morse Commission posed — whether any large dam displacing tens of thousands can meet the Bank's own rules — has never been answered.

**Yacyreta (Argentina–Paraguay, 1979–2004).** A binational dam on the Paraná River, treaty-signed 1973, financed by the Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) from 1979. What happened: cost estimates climbed from $2.5 billion toward $10.5–12 billion (independent estimates ran to $15 billion with interest); construction suspended in 1983 (Falklands war, recession), resumed under a 1986 loan; the 1992 Action Plan promised resettlement and rehabilitation; in 1994 the reservoir was filled to its operating level with mitigation incomplete — in apparent breach of the loan agreement; Paraguayan and Argentine complainants went to the Inspection Panel (the Panel's first investigation, 1997) but management's assurances were accepted; Argentine payments were suspended in 1995; a second request (2002) produced the Panel's May 6, 2004 report finding four policy violations on fourteen counts (environmental assessment, resettlement, indigenous peoples, project supervision). Senator Richard Lugar's April 20, 2004 letter to Wolfensohn — from the chair of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee — demanded a forensic audit of where the $8 billion in overruns went. The audit never happened. Argentine president Carlos Menem had already given the project its permanent name: "un monumento a la corrupción" — a monument to corruption; Paraguayans spoke of the "Yacyreta millionaires." The Bank lent $878 million over 1979–2002 and closed the file with a $210 million pledge the Panel never verified; the dam operates at roughly 60 percent of its potential. A fully documented case of oversight existing and failing twice.

**Chixoy (Guatemala, 1972–1989).** The Bank lent $15 million (1972), $4.2 million (1976) and a supplemental $44.4 million for Guatemala's largest hydroelectric dam — through the early 1980s, as the Guatemalan state carried out genocide against Maya communities. Between 1980 and 1983 the army and civil patrols massacred the Maya-Achí villages of the Rio Negro basin — roughly 400 people killed, many at the Rio Negro massacres of February-March 1982, committed by forces including those patrolling the reservoir area; the community's resistance had begun with refusal to accept resettlement terms. The dam's legacy is recorded in the Inter-American Court of Human Rights' 2022 judgment (Case 12.578) finding Guatemala internationally responsible for the massacres. Reparations: in 2014 — thirty years on — the Guatemalan government agreed to about $154 million over fifteen years, a commitment extracted partly by a US appropriations rider conditioning Bank and IDB funding. The Bank's own safeguard regime of that era had no mechanism for "stop lending while the borrower commits massacres"; it still doesn't.

**Lesotho Highlands Water Project (1987–2002).** Southern Africa's largest infrastructure scheme — dams and tunnels selling water to South Africa (apartheid-era South Africa: the project's first phases were agreed while the regime was under sanctions; the Bank lent for it anyway). The corruption case became unique in development history: Lesotho's own courts convicted the project's chief executive, Masupha Sole, for accepting bribes, and prosecuted international contractors for paying them — Acres International (Canada), Lahmeyer International (Germany), Impregilo, Spie Batignolles, Dumez, ABB, Sogreah, ED. Züblin, and others; convictions included British, French, German, Swiss, South African, and Italian firms. The Bank's behavior became the secondary scandal: it had known of allegations since 1994; when Lesotho suspended Sole, the Bank's country director (Praful Patel) wrote to the government complaining of the suspension; the Bank waited eleven months after Acres's conviction (thirty-two after charging) to debar, and awarded new contracts to indicted firms while proceedings pended. An OECD working group and Transparency International documented the gap between the Bank's anti-corruption rhetoric and its treatment of the only African government ever to prosecute a multinational bribery case. Acquittals and plea-deals aside (several convictions were overturned on appeal), the case stands as the inverse of partnership: the borrower enforced the law; the Bank obstructed.

**Polonoroeste (Brazil, 1981–1985).** The Northwest Regional Development Program: a Bank-financed (roughly $434 million) highway colonization scheme opening Rondônia's Amazon frontier. Roughly a million migrants followed the road; deforestation and disease followed the migrants; the Bank's own 1987 review conceded the indigenous protection plan had failed (it had earlier rated the project's indigenous component satisfactory). The Bank halted disbursements mid-course (1985) — an early admission that growth projects could destroy what they claimed to develop — and the episode seeded the environmental safeguard policies adopted in 1984–89. The road is still called BR-364; Rondônia's forest is largely gone.

**Transmigration (Indonesia, 1976–1992).** Seven loans totaling roughly $560 million supporting the relocation of millions from Java to Sumatra, Kalimantan, and Sulawesi. The Bank's own 1994 review conceded "major and probably irreversible" impacts on indigenous peoples (the Kubu of Sumatra most directly) and catalyzed deforestation at rates approaching 300,000 hectares a year; Javanese transmigrants received systematically larger plots than local communities, seeding horizontal conflict that flared in Kalimantan in the late 1990s. The official program was halted in 1986; the Bank's involvement was rated "highly unsatisfactory" by its own evaluators. The forests of the outer islands are the transmigration monument.

**Nam Theun 2 (Laos, 2005– ).** The Bank's return to big dams after Narmada was engineered around safeguards: the $1.45 billion project (a Lao-Thai joint venture with French and Thai shareholders) displaced about 6,200 people directly and affected tens of thousands downstream, with compensation obligations projected for a generation. Supporters call it the model of safeguards done right; critics call it the managed displacement the model legitimized. The Nakai Plateau resettlement villages remain a reference point in every safeguards debate.

**Bujagali (Uganda, 1994–2012).** Eighteen years from first study to commissioning: the original developer (AES Nile Power) withdrew in 2003 amid corruption allegations and the Aga Khan's Industrial Promotion Services took over; the Inspection Panel twice accepted requests (the 2001 request led to Uganda's first panel case history); the Bank left and returned; the dam finally delivered power in 2012 at a cost of nearly $900 million. The saga is the case study in how mega-projects absorb accountability rather than respond to it.

**Cochabamba (Bolivia, 1997–2000).** The Bank's country assistance strategy conditioned support on water-sector privatization; the concession went to Aguas del Tunari — a consortium of Bechtel (55 percent), Edison, Abengoa, and Bolivian capital. Water rates rose 50–200 percent; in December 1999–April 2000 the city revolted (the Water War); a seventeen-year-old, Víctor Hugo Daza, was shot dead; martial law was declared; the government annulled the contract. In November 2001 the consortium filed at ICSID: $50 million claimed ($25 million in damages, $25 million in lost profits) on an investment under $1 million. Jurisdiction was upheld in October 2005; the claim was withdrawn in January 2006 for two bolivianos — about thirty cents — after a global campaign (Earthjustice, the Democracy Center, Public Citizen; three hundred organizations in forty-three countries; a rejected petition). The episode founded the global anti-water-privatization movement and re-priced ICSID's legitimacy in the global South.

**Porgera (Papua New Guinea).** The gold mine (Barrick, via Placer Dome) — security forces guarding it admitted to a string of killings and hundreds of house-burnings in 2005 alone; mine waste continued down the river in a practice a court later ruled illegal; Norway's sovereign wealth fund divested on its ethics council's advice (the Fund held NOK 1,274 million of Barrick stock). The community's compensation case ran for over a decade; in 2024 Barrick settled a related class action for a fraction of the claims.

**Dinant (Honduras).** IFC lent to Corporación Dinant, a palm-oil and biofuel agribusiness, in the Bajo Agún valley — where dozens of farmers were killed in land conflicts from 2009 onward. The Bank's own Compliance Advisor Ombudsman found IFC failed to verify the client's account of the violence and pressed for remedy; IFC divested quietly. A fully documented case of an accountability office working exactly as designed and remedy still not delivered.

**Tata Mundra (India) and *Jam v. IFC* (2010–2019).** The IFC lent $450 million to the Tata Ultra Mega Power Project, a 4,000 MW coastal coal plant in Gujarat. Fishing communities' livelihoods collapsed under the plant's warm-water outflows and ash; their grievance mechanism petition was rejected by IFC management in 2013; they sued in US courts. *Jam v. International Finance Corporation* reached the Supreme Court, which on February 27, 2019 held (7–1) that international-organization immunity is near-absolute and the IFC's partial waiver did not reach the claims. The case settled confidentially in 2021. The legal precedent stands: the institution's victims have no forum but its own.

**Kariba REDD+ (Zimbabwe, 2015–2023).** The world's flagship forest-carbon project, second-largest in the world at launch: a Swiss developer (South Pole, CEO Renat Heuberger) and a local partner (Carbon Green Africa, Steve Wentzel) projected 52 million credits over thirty years from a forest that wasn't disappearing — an analyst (Elias Ayrey of Renoster) found the baseline inflated about 96 percent, roughly 23–27 million effectively phantom credits were sold to Volkswagen, Gucci, Nestlé, Porsche, Delta, L'Oréal, McKinsey, the Cannes Film Festival, and Australian zoos before the collapse; a whistleblower (Dirk Muench) filed internally and got a "case closed" answer; the verifier (Verra) certified further credits after questions were raised. Of the roughly €40–57 million claimed transferred, about €6 million could be accounted for; South Pole terminated the project in October 2023; Zimbabwe voided all carbon-offset agreements and claimed half of future proceeds. The Bank's own carbon initiatives — the Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (its flagship had retired only a fraction of its capital as credits, with an evaluator finding maize-yield claims false) — proceed in the wreckage, though the Bank announced (December 2023) ambitious carbon-growth plans anyway (fifteen countries, $2.5 billion potential).

**Bridge International Academies (Kenya; 2013–2024).** The for-profit school chain (NewGlobe; founders Shannon May and Jay Kimmelman) that IFC backed with $13.5 million received child sexual abuse reports in 2013, 2016, and 2017 — and did nothing with them. The full anatomy **[INTERNAL-FOUND]**: the Bank's Compliance Advisor Ombudsman received complaints in 2018, opened an investigation, and in December 2023 reported that IFC had failed its environmental-and-social due-diligence duties; that CSA cases by teaching staff numbered at least 21 (a client list ran to seventy-plus); and that in June 2020 IFC had signed a wide-ranging confidentiality agreement with the company over the CAO's objection. Leaked September 2020 meeting notes record the founders discussing with IFC's asset-management chief William Sonneborn how to protect a funding round and a Rwanda government contract — including removing the CAO's leadership ("fire Osvaldo Gratacós"; "neutralize [investigator] Daniel Adler"). Gratacós, the CAO head, was replaced on December 31, 2020 after six years (two Bank sources said he was offered his job back if he fired Adler; he declined); Adler, the investigator, was suspended in August 2023 and remains so; IFC divested in March 2022; the Board published the CAO report in March 2024 only under US congressional pressure (Senators Warren, Welch, and Waters); President Banga's apology email conceded "protocols were not followed... mistakes were made" while naming no one; no victim compensation followed (a general Kenyan psychosocial program was the remedy); the promised external investigation's terms remained undefined a year on. The investigator who did his job is suspended; the executives who managed the cover-up kept their careers.

**Java 9&10 and the financial-intermediary loophole.** IFC's $15.36 million equity in Indonesia's Hana Bank (2020) flowed, within a year, to two new coal plants (Java 9&10 — 2,000 MW, an estimated 250 million tons of CO₂ over their lives) — despite the Bank's exclusion policies, because lending through commercial banks sits outside project safeguards. The campaign-file record: Inclusive Development International's 2017 *Disaster for Us and the Planet* traced at least 41 new coal projects funded by IFC-backed intermediaries since the 2013 coal ban; the Philippines RCBC case (IFC equity; eleven coal plants; a class-action-style CAO complaint) became the first compliance investigation of IFC's indirect climate contribution; the Green Equity Approach (October 2020) — zero coal by 2030 for equity clients — was closed in April 2023 after the Hana case; and the October 2023 *Blowing Smoke* report finds IFC still exposed to **68.3 gigawatts of coal capacity — nearly double Germany's entire fleet — across 39 projects in China, Indonesia, and Cambodia**, with bond-underwriting and "captive coal" loopholes still open and existing clients still financing coal developers. The documented fix-list — extend the approach to oil and gas, close the underwriting channel, full prior disclosure, remedy for past harms — is on the record alongside the Bank's partial reforms.

**The geothermal FPIC case (Wae Sano).** A companion Indonesia case showing the safeguards machinery operating in reverse — catching the Bank's exit, not its entry. The World Bank's Geothermal Upstream Development Program financed early-stage drilling across Indonesia's volcano arc; on Flores's Wae Sano site, the indigenous community's free, prior, and informed consent process collapsed — community members rejected the project, reports documented intimidation, and the Bank withdrew from the site in December 2023 (the German development bank KfW followed at its neighboring Poco Leok site in November 2024). The documented after-chain is worse than the exit: in October 2024, four residents protesting the project's continuation were beaten and detained, and a journalist covering the confrontation was assaulted. A geothermal well is small against the mega-project casebook; the sequence — consent process, Bank exit, violence after the exit — is the safeguard system's limits in miniature: it can stop a project, but it cannot protect the people afterward.

## 22. The Scandal File: The Institution Against Itself

**The consultant-trust-fund scandal (1997–1999).** The trigger for the modern integrity apparatus: Bank officials and consultants rigged contracts financed by Swedish trust funds (the SIDA Consultant Trust Funds) in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Sri Lanka. The Bank's investigation implicated around ninety individuals across twenty-eight countries; prosecutions and disciplinary actions followed; the Bank created the Department of Institutional Integrity (April 2001), adopted its first real sanctions procedures, and learned to debar — though rarely to recover.

**The Siemens case (2009).** The German industrial giant paid roughly $100 million in bribes on a Moscow transport project; the Bank debarred it for two years. The moment the sanctions system touched a blue-chip Western firm, the system's double standard became visible: Siemens's debarment was negotiable from day one.

**Padma Bridge (Bangladesh, 2010–2017).** The Bank's defining stand, told at full length. The project: a $2.9 billion bridge over the Padma River, the largest infrastructure undertaking in Bangladesh's history, with the Bank as lead financier ($1.2 billion). In March 2011 the Bank received corruption allegations from a confidential source; by September 2011 it had wiretap-derived evidence of a conspiracy to bribe Bangladeshi officials — communications between executives of the Canadian engineering firm SNC-Lavalin and of EC Harris (later part of Jacobs) describing arrangements for kickbacks on supervision contracts. The Bank suspended the loan in October 2011; presented its evidence to the government in April 2012; and on June 29, 2012 — when the government's response was insufficient — **canceled**, declaring that the Bank "cannot, should not, and will not turn a blind eye" to credible evidence of corruption, citing its obligations to its board and its borrowers. The after-play: a September 2012 agreement on a framework for re-engagement (leave for implicated officials, an external panel, a special anti-corruption team); SNC-Lavalin admitted culpability in June 2013 and accepted a ten-year debarment of the parent and about one hundred affiliates under a Negotiated Resolution Agreement (alongside its separate Canadian conviction on Libya-related charges); the Canadian prosecution of the executives collapsed in February 2017 when a judge excluded the wiretap evidence; Bangladesh's own Anti-Corruption Commission cleared the officials; a Canadian parliamentary officer and Transparency International called the outcome a failure of political will. Bangladesh built the bridge with its own funds, completing it in June 2022 at roughly double the original cost — a fact each side cites forever: the Bank's defenders as vindication of principle (corruption was real; the money moved elsewhere), its critics as proof the Bank's leverage was the price of the bridge. (The wiretap saga had a second life in Canadian law: the World Bank's refusal to hand its investigators' records to the RCMP produced *World Bank Group v. Wallace*, the 2016 Supreme Court immunity ruling — Part II §9.)

**The Wolfowitz affair (2005–2007).** The full anatomy, because it is the only complete expulsion in the institution's history and its lessons were never institutionalized. The appointment (March–June 2005): the Bush administration's deputy defense secretary, architect of the Iraq War, was nominated over the visible resistance of the staff (a petition) and much of Europe; he arrived promising an Africa-first, governance-heavy presidency. The misconduct: (1) the Riza arrangement — he directed the Bank's ethics committee (whose advice he had sought and then overruled in implementation) to arrange the transfer of his companion Shaha Riza to the State Department with compensation totaling $180,000 in 2005 (a $47,300 raise against a 12 percent staff ceiling) and $193,590 in 2006 (against a 3.7 percent ceiling), plus a promotion guarantee on return — a package the board's ad hoc committee later called inconsistent with his obligations; (2) the political staffing — allies from the Bush Pentagon and campaign (Kevin Kellems, Robin Cleveland) installed in senior vice-presidential roles at roughly $250,000 with opaque selection processes; (3) the Daboub affair — his budget director, Juan José Daboub, ordered the deletion of family-planning and reproductive-health references from Bank strategy documents for Madagascar and from the Bank's own health materials, and attempted to purge references to climate change from a major strategy speech — conduct the board committee found to have occurred on Wolfowitz's watch; (4) the retaliation climate — senior staff who resisted were isolated. The endgame: the Staff Association's formal no-confidence (June 4, 2007); the ad hoc board committee's findings (June 14); Wolfowitz's defenders (African finance ministers, notably) rallying on governance grounds; and the negotiated exit — resignation announced May 17, effective June 30, with the board's statement noting both his leadership on Africa and the ethics findings. Zoellick was nominated May 30 and took office July 1, 2007. Postscript: virtually every structural reform his expulsion was said to enable — a stronger ethics regime, a real anti-corruption strategy with protection for staff — was diluted within five years. Pattern Six, full cycle.

**Doing Business (2001–2021).** The flagship ranking began as technocratic genius — one number compressing a country's business regulations, used by everyone from investors to presidents — and died as a data-integrity scandal, told in full because it is the institution's single most instructive self-harm. The founding: the team (Simeon Djankov among its leaders) built the index from expert surveys; the first report (2003–04); the ranking soon became national policy targets (India's "rank 142 to 63" campaign; the World Bank's own research later showed a *negative* association between ranking-chasing and actual firm entry). The first crack: January 2018 — chief economist Paul Romer (Nobel laureate-to-be) told the Wall Street Journal that methodological changes had been manipulated for political reasons, citing Chile (rankings moved with its governments); he apologized to Chile within days, retracted his blog, and resigned January 24, 2018; the board ordered an external audit of the indicators' integrity. The detonation: an internal review flagged data irregularities in 2020; the Bank suspended publication; the independent law-firm investigation (WilmerHale, engaged January 2021, reported September 15, 2021) found that for the Doing Business 2018 report, changes had been made to China's data (across Starting a Business, Getting Credit, Paying Taxes) lifting its score and ranking to 78th, under direct pressure from the office of then-president Kim and chief executive Georgieva (a "bit for multilateralism" was the reported phrase — the pressure arriving as the Bank sought Chinese support for the capital increase), and that Doing Business 2020 carried improper changes benefiting Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Azerbaijan, and Jordan; that the then-chief economist (Shanta Devarajan) and the then-director had at minimum failed to stop it; that there was no evidence the board ordered the 2020 changes; and that the institution lacked a safe environment for raising concerns. The same day, the Bank discontinued Doing Business entirely. Georgieva — by then IMF managing director — faced an IMF board review (October 11, 2021) and survived (the Europeans backed her; the US and Japan reportedly favored removal). The replacement, B-READY, launched October 2024 with deliberate design changes: no headline ranking, three pillars, peer review of every economy's data, and a data-use license restricting ranking-construction. The lessons one can state without controversy: the ranking had become too politically valuable not to game; the people who gamed it were promoted (one to the IMF's top job); and the reform addressed the instrument, not the incentive.

**The Malpass climate comments (2022).** September 20, 2022, Climate Week NYC: asked by a former Trump Treasury official (David Toomey, at a panel) whether he accepted that fossil-fuel burning was warming the planet, Malpass demurred — "I'm not a scientist" — before Al Gore's public criticism forced a written clarification. The European climate ministers' open call for his resignation followed; he served out five more months and announced his resignation February 15, 2023, effective June 30. Substantively his presidency had delivered the COVID surge ($157 billion in fifteen months; the vaccine envelope scaled from $12 billion to $20 billion); the episode remains the purest instance of a presidency ending over a sentence rather than a program.

**The Chaves case (2019) and the harassment file.** The Bank's Administrative Tribunal substantiated sexual-harassment findings against Rodrigo Chaves — a 27-year senior official — covering conduct from 2008–2013 toward multiple women: "relentless and unwanted advances," sexual innuendo, unwelcome physical conduct; twenty-seven witnesses were interviewed. The Tribunal criticized the institution's own HR for characterizing proven sexual misconduct as mere "misconduct" ("the undisputed facts legally amount to sexual harassment," the Tribunal wrote, citing testimony from the then-vice president for human resources). He was demoted (salary frozen three years, responsibilities removed) rather than dismissed, resigned November 30, 2019, was banned from rehire in January 2021 — and was inaugurated President of Costa Rica on May 8, 2022 (his April 2022 apology video acknowledged "actions that... caused discomfort" fifteen years earlier), a head of state under the Bank's own facility-access restrictions. Around the case sits the institution's pattern: El País's 2018 finding that one in four female Bank employees surveyed reported feeling sexually harassed; FY18 accusations more than triple the prior year's (33 vs 11); the 1980s-era ombuds handling that dismissed an early complaint for lack of witnesses; the 2019 Action Plan (a dedicated anti-harassment coordinator, shortened investigation timelines from 145 to 110 days, anonymous case publication) — and the structural tell that the Tribunal's remedial orders have historically run to legal costs, not reinstatement or discipline of the managers who enabled.

**Karen Hudes (1986–2007).** The senior counsel (21 years in the Legal Department) who reported a corrupt takeover of the Philippines' second-largest bank and got destroyed for it. Her account, documented in her later court filings and the congressional record: in 1999 she asked the country director to sign a letter warning the Philippine government that a loan could not be disbursed without a board waiver (conditionality unmet); the director reassigned her instead; the internal audit department declined to correct a flawed supervision report or an incomplete integrity referral; two days after she briefed the Audit Committee on the cover-up she was reprimanded and placed on probation. The Audit Committee chair requested an inquiry into the Integrity Department; the Senate Foreign Relations Committee (Lugar, Bayh, Leahy) sent three letters. She was terminated in late July 2007 — days before the Wolfowitz expulsion consumed the institution. What happened next is the legal fortress in action: the Administrative Council (COGAM's chair and the board's dean) requested her reinstatement on August 20, 2009, after her February 2009 disclosure of "ongoing control lapses" to the Audit Committee chairman (the Bank hired KPMG for an internal-controls audit); on September 2, 2009, the Bank barred her from the building; the Zoellick presidency declined to implement the reinstatement request; the Staff Association's lawyer was fired in the same season. Her US court claims (a Sarbanes-Oxley whistleblower complaint with the Labor Department, filed October 2009) were dismissed on immunity grounds (*Hudes v. IBRD*, DOL ARB No. 2010-SOX-00012; D.C. District Court dismissal for lack of jurisdiction). She died in October 2022. The case file connects directly to the tribunal record: the institution that cannot be sued also does not have to rehire the truthful.

**The STC fraud case (2020s).** An internal investigation substantiated that a staff member ran a scheme across dozens of projects and three countries using short-term-consultant contracts (roughly ninety-five STCs, thirty-five country-office contracts, thirty-plus projects, five trust funds) — the shadow-workforce channel exploited from inside. The discipline (termination and restitution) was imposed; the systemic fix — controls over the consultant channel — arrived only when the 2026 hiring freeze shut the channel entirely, with all the collateral damage Part VII describes.

## 23. The Corruption Networks

**The Sattar network (Malawi; live as of this writing).** **[ON TRIAL — presumed innocent; proceedings continuing November 18–21, 2026]** The single largest live corruption case touching World Bank-financed operations. Zuneth Sattar, a British-Malawian businessman born in 1981, built a procurement empire selling to the Malawian security state through his companies (Xaviar Ltd, Malachitte FZE and affiliates): roughly $56 million in contracts between December 2020 and September 2021 — $28 million in anti-riot equipment, $7.8 million in police rations, $20 million in armored vehicles, routed through UAE entities. The UK National Crime Agency arrested him in October 2021; he was charged under the Bribery Act (eighteen counts) and tried at Southwark Crown Court (case 00NS5051220) beginning September 6, 2026 (a "fitness to plead" hearing in March 2026 rejected a defense claim that a brain injury left him unfit; the Crown proceeds with medical oversight). The alleged recipient network, named in court and reporting: Vice President Saulos Chilima (charged in Malawi; the charges dropped by the Director of Public Prosecutions in May 2024, four weeks before he died in a plane crash in June 2024), Chief of Staff Prince Kapondamgaga (suspended), Inspector General George Kainja (fired; banned from the US in April 2024 along with other officials), former Anti-Corruption Bureau director Reyneck Matemba, Brigadier Dan Kuwali, procurement director John Suzi Banda, police lawyer Mwabi Kaluba. The institutional ecology: the ACB's director Martha Chizuma (her interrogation audio leaked in January 2022; arrested in December 2022; left office mid-2024) — an ACB executive, Issa Njauju, was murdered in 2015; a tax officer probing related flows, Alinafe Bolongwe, was murdered in February 2022; the Attorney General (Thabo Chakaka Nyirenda) refused the UK's mutual-legal-assistance request; a $150 million police-and-military procurement saga was quietly dropped in January 2025; Malawi has no extradition treaty with the UK. The World Bank froze its Malawi pipeline over the scandal. The trial (prosecution led by Edward Brown KC (King's Counsel, the senior British trial-lawyer rank); defense by Simon Farrell KC; before Judge Christopher Hehir; the arrest-phase judge was Tan Ikram) continues. The anomaly in one sentence: the businessman is tried in London; the institutions that bought from him face no equivalent reckoning at home.

**The trader lane (Chad and beyond).** **[CONVICTED — corporate pleas]** Glencore is the only entity in this casebook with criminal convictions on both sides of the Atlantic: May 2022, a US Department of Justice resolution exceeding $1.1 billion (a decade of bribery and market manipulation in its oil business); June 2022, a guilty plea by its UK subsidiary to Serious Fraud Office bribery charges covering Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, Ivory Coast, Nigeria, and South Sudan — the SFO case originating in the bribery of officials across African oil markets, including agents of Sonara in Cameroon (the refinery of the very pipeline the Bank financed). The Chad machine, documented in an OECD commodity-trading study: prepayment loans of $300 million (2013), then $600 million, then a **$1.45 billion syndicated loan in 2014** (Glencore with Citibank UK, Deutsche Bank, Natixis, and Crédit Agricole; pricing at Libor plus 6.6–7.5 percent) financing Chad's purchase of its own 25 percent Doba-stake — after which roughly 90 percent of the field's 2016 revenue flowed to Glencore; Glencore had been the field's effective monopsony buyer since 2010; winner selection processes were visible to no other bidder; the loan was twice restructured (2015 — converted, under HIPC conditionality, into long-term claims whose present value *increased*; 2018 — with oil-price triggers); Chad requested a payment suspension in 2020. The wider field: Gunvor's $750 million of Congolese prepayments (2011–12) carrying $31.9 million in commissions to politically exposed agents' Swiss accounts (a $95 million Swiss penalty in 2019; BNP Paribas financing of $380–400 million); Vitol's $135–164 million in FCPA penalties (2020, covering Mexico, Brazil, Ecuador, and Cameroon); Trafigura's use of a sanctioned middleman in South Sudan; fourteen resource-backed-lending countries, ten in debt distress after the 2014 oil crash. The Bank's own 2007 report found Congo's state oil trader (SNPC) selling 5–6 percent below market; the Bank lent anyway. This is the shadow financing system: traders lend where the Bank's safeguards cannot follow, price the political risk at 7–15 percent, and hold sovereigns' own resources as collateral — while the Bank's preferred-creditor claims sit legally ahead of everyone's.

**The Chinese contractor funnel.** **[WB-SANCTIONED; documented pattern]** The corporate record first: China Communications Construction Company and China State Construction Engineering Corporation were debarred in 2009 (eight and six years) for bid-rigging in Philippines road projects (the National Roads Improvement and Management Program); the Bank's successor doctrine extended sanctions to CCCC Limited's affiliates through 2017; China Railway Construction Corporation and two subsidiaries took a nine-month debarment in 2019 (Georgia's East–West Highway). Then the reversal: a June 2026 US Congressional Research Service report (CRS R48983) finds the **five largest winners of World Bank civil-works contracts in 2025 are all Chinese state-owned enterprises** — China Civil Engineering Construction Corporation, Sichuan Road and Bridge Corporation, TBEA Engineering, CCCC, and China Railway Construction Engineering. The distribution: the US GAO estimates China takes roughly 3 percent of contracts by count but about 29 percent by dollars (2013–2022), with the known limitation that borrower-identified registration ignores beneficial ownership; Devex's FY2020 ledger had China at 23.6 percent of contract value; the Center for Global Development (CGD) finds the top ten Chinese contractors won about 60 percent of 2020 contracts, eight of ten state-owned. The surrounding record (C4ADS's *Bulldozed*, May 2025): labor abuses in a Sri Lankan irrigation tunnel; a Kenyan tax-evasion conviction (about $8 million, August 2024) routed through shell companies; Malaysia's 2018 suspension of a CCCC railroad over corruption and overbilling; a Bangladesh blacklisting of China Harbour Engineering over bribery; and the financial-complicity detail — Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and Merrill Lynch held CCCC stock through its debarment years, and Deutsche Bank signed a cooperation agreement with CSCEC's Middle East arm in October 2024 while US defense authorities list the companies as Chinese military contractors. The Bank's position — that Chinese contractors deliver value for money — is not refuted by any of this; it simply coexists with it.

**Adani.** **[INDICTED — trial scheduled; denied]** The November 20, 2024 US indictment (Eastern District of New York) charges Gautam Adani, Sagar Adani, Vneet Jaain and five others with securities and wire fraud: an alleged $250 million-plus in bribes to Indian officials (including, per the indictment's account, a Andhra Pradesh-era arrangement) to secure solar-power contracts projected to yield $2 billion-plus in profits, alongside $2 billion-plus in syndicated loans and $1 billion-plus in bond offerings raised on allegedly false statements. Fallout to date: Kenya canceled a $2 billion airport project and a $736 million power purchase agreement; TotalEnergies paused its joint ventures; Abu Dhabi's International Holding Company maintained; the domestic proceedings of India's Securities and Exchange Board grind on. The Bank connection is reputational rather than financial: no direct IFC exposure has surfaced — but the Bank's own 2013 ports flagship report held up Adani's Mundra port as the model public-private partnership (its cover credited the Adani photo collection), and the Bank's container-port performance index continues to rank Adani's terminals. The development establishment's showcase partners are one indictment away from becoming case studies.

**Kenya's Arid Lands project.** **[WB-INTERNAL-FOUND; congressional testimony]** The Kenya Arid Lands Support project — a community-driven development operation in Kenya's north — was rated "satisfactory" for fourteen consecutive years (an "exemplary" rating in 2007). The Bank's own Integrity Vice Presidency ultimately found **roughly 62 percent of about 28,000 sampled transactions suspicious or fraudulent**. Sworn congressional testimony (2017, House Financial Services) from the anthropologist who ran the community-facilitation study accompanying the investigation — and who validated the findings with forensic digit analysis (the Benford's-law-style test returning p = 0.0227) — stated that "the same patterns [continue] on the ground today." The institution's supervisory apparatus had rated a fraud-saturated project satisfactory for a decade and a half. The Bank's counter — the Volcker Triggers system, flagging roughly 5 percent of the pipeline — is itself the measure of the gap between detected and actual.

**Cambodia's microfinance boom.** **[INTERNAL-FOUND]** The February 2022 complaint by LICADHO and Equitable Cambodia to the Bank's accountability office — ruled eligible in April 2022 and elevated to a compliance investigation in June 2023 — alleges that six IFC-backed microfinance institutions (ACLEDA, Amret, Prasac, Hattha Bank, LOLC, and Sathapana — roughly 75 percent of the Cambodian market; IFC exposure around $400 million since the late 2000s, direct and through funds) and four financing institutions engaged in predatory and deceptive lending that forced land sales: the documented mechanism runs from over-indebted households to "negotiated" sales convened by village chiefs (who received small payments), with land titles withheld until agreement, to indigenous communities surrendering communal land titles (ICLTs) to service debts. The complaint survey recorded 85 of 717 households selling gold, animals, or vehicles under debt pressure. A parallel complaint against the Dutch development bank's client relationships proceeds in the Netherlands. The IFC board's instruction to review the investigation decision was quietly dropped. The case turns the microfinance success story inside out: near-universal credit as a land-transfer mechanism.

**The procurement and sanctions ledger.** Cumulative output of the modern sanctions system (1996– ): roughly 1,250 debarred entities and individuals from around 120 countries; fraud the modal offense (about 64 percent of recent first-tier cases); settlements now matching contested cases in number (19 and 19 in FY2025); enforcement volume below pre-pandemic levels; cross-debarment operating across the major MDBs (the AIIB joining most recently, cross-debarring a Moscow rail-signals company in August 2026 that the Bank had debarred through 2032 for collusion and corruption). Referrals to national authorities proceed quietly (FY2023: Germany, the DRC, the EU's anti-fraud office, Tajikistan, Pakistan, Kenya, Egypt). What the system does not do: recover. Debarment removes the corrupt from future contracts; restitution is exceptional; and the stolen money stays stolen. The FY25 settlement list alone — a Yunnan machinery maker, an Indonesian consultancy group, Indian software firms, an Azerbaijani auto dealer, a Nigerian engineer, a Dutch NGO (conditional, for fraud in the Central African Republic) — reads as a census of the ordinary, not the spectacular.

## 24. The Country Files

**Rwanda (1990–1998).** The grimmest entry in the ledger. The Bank and Fund maintained structural-adjustment programs with the Habyarimana regime through 1993 as it armed for genocide. The documented mechanics: the 1990 SAP (imposed the week the RPF invasion began) mandated a 40 percent devaluation and import liberalization — which eased the regime's hard-currency purchases of weapons from Bulgaria, Albania, Egypt, and South Africa; bilateral balance-of-payments aid was diverted to arms; the regime additionally borrowed roughly $250 million domestically (from the central bank, the Development Bank, the Social Security Fund, and the National Insurance Company) as the killing was prepared. The Bank's own evaluator (the Operations Evaluation Department's 1994 country assessment) recorded that it was "not clear why IDA continued to design such complex projects until the eve of the genocide," rated the pre-genocide program unsatisfactory with negligible impact, and noted the institution had continued lending while its own staff could not supervise in the field. The successor government inherited the debt — about $1 billion — and chose to honor it (finance minister Donald Kaberuka: the debts were sovereign); the 1998 relief framework refinanced arrears to all multilateral creditors "other than the World Bank, AfDB, and IFAD." The institutions exempted themselves from a genocide-successor state's relief. Rwanda reached HIPC completion in April 2005; today the Bank is again its largest multilateral creditor (about $3.3–3.8 billion of a $9.5 billion debt stock) — and, in a financial-engineering footnote, a recent Rwandan sustainability-linked bond embeds $10 million of Bank on-lent loans as credit enhancement. The system closes its own loops.

**Zaire/Mobutu.** The IMF's own records document the pillage contemporaneously: family withdrawals of $71 million (1977), $65 million (1978), and $95 million (1979); a 1979 parliamentary commission finding $150 million illegally drawn from the central bank; the IMF's own Zaire adviser, Erwin Blumenthal, resigning in frustration; roughly fifty ruling-class companies withholding $300 million abroad; Erwin Blumenthal's memo concluding the money would never be repaid. The pattern: 1976 brink of bankruptcy; 1977 an IMF stand-by ($54 million) granted the same week Moroccan troops flown in by the US and France blunted the Shaba invasion; 1979 the largest extended facility in sub-Saharan history ($420 million) — while the regime's extraction continued; Bank and Fund adjustment lending repaid private creditors until roughly $14 billion of private debt had become public debt; a Bank study identifying 149 major embezzlers. The Bank's own recognition of "governance" as a lending criterion arrived in 1993. Mobutu died in 1997, in exile, in Morocco; the debt remained.

**The client-state pattern.** The ledger repeats across regimes: loans to Duvalier's Haiti (roughly 40 percent of the debt still on the books in 2000 — debt incurred while the father and then the son ruled through terror); to Somoza's Nicaragua until the 1979 revolution; to Marcos's Philippines (the Bank's own 1980 Ascher memorandum prized the regime's "technocratic pillar" while its cronies — the coconut levy, the sugar monopolies, the behest loans — captured the banking system; the Philippine Commission on Good Government spent decades recovering what the regime stole); to Pinochet's Chile after 1973 (lending resumed in 1976 as the Chicago Boys consolidated; the Bank's staff documented the miracle more than the torturers); to Suharto's Indonesia (the 30 percent leakage memo; the frozen account scandals; the Bank's own 1994-97 governance lending that arrived, critics noted, only after the family's enrichment was complete); to apartheid South Africa until 1966 (the last loan, an electricity utility credit, came after Albert Luthuli and Martin Luther King had called for sanctions); to Myanmar's junta (lending through the 1970s and 1980s; interruption around the 1988 coup; a mid-1990s re-engagement; arrears; clearance in 2012); to Dos Santos's Angola — roughly $180 million across 1992–99 to a government whose elite was, by the IMF's own contemporaneous accounting, siphoning oil revenues through the state diamond and fuel monopolies, the cleanest documented case of the Bank lending *because* a country was resource-rich and reform-proof — to the Shah's Iran until 1979 and, after a long freeze, to the Islamic Republic from 1991 (seven projects, $847.4 million, approved over US objections in 1991–93; G7 consensus blocked further lending from 1993 to 2000; the coalition broke in May 2000 with $232 million of health and sewerage loans approved 21–3). The common structure: the Bank lends to states; states are sometimes run by thieves; and the money's destination is knowable only in retrospect — because Article IV §10, the political-prohibition clause, is read to forbid asking the question in advance.

**Haiti (2010–2013).** The earthquake of January 12, 2010 (an estimated 316,000 dead; 1.5 million displaced; the Presidential Palace, the ministries, the cathedral — the state itself — destroyed). The recovery architecture: a Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (led by the UN, the Bank, the European Union, and the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean) priced damage at $7.9 billion; the Action Plan for the Recovery and Development of Haiti requested $11.5 billion; the March 31, 2010 New York donors' conference pledged $5.3 billion for 2010–12 plus $3.8 billion for 2013–20 (totaling, with later additions, about $10.4 billion). The Interim Haiti Recovery Commission — co-chaired by UN Special Envoy Bill Clinton and Prime Minister Jean-Max Bellerive — held its first meeting mid-2010 and approved projects; its 18-month mandate lapsed unrenewed under the next president. The Bank-administered Haiti Reconstruction Fund (Clinton pressing the Bank to serve as fiscal agent; the Bank's management fee later became a public friction between the two) received a fraction of pledges (about $280 million by the reckoning of its evaluator; Brazil the largest contributor). The OSE (the UN's own Office of the Special Envoy) tracking ledger, three years on: 94 percent of humanitarian money disbursed but only 51 percent of recovery pledges; 99 percent of relief and 86 percent of recovery money bypassed Haitian public financial systems; under 7 percent of bilateral recovery funds passed through the government; less than one percent of humanitarian funding reached Haitian organizations directly; budget support arrived more than half in the last two months of the fiscal year. The Bank's own contribution was respectable ($479 million in total support, plus a debt write-off) but miniature against the need. The parallel-governance verdict stands: a reconstruction directed around the state it claimed to rebuild, with the Haitian people as spectators at their own recovery.

**Afghanistan (2001–2021).** Twenty years, well over $16 billion through the Bank-administered Afghanistan Reconstruction Trust Fund (the ARTF, capitalized by dozens of donors and run by the Bank since 2002): the National Solidarity Program's thirty-some-thousand elected community-development councils; the health program (SEHAT, later SEHAT-MANDI) that kept 1,800-plus facilities running and became, by the war's end, the country's only functioning health network; education, irrigation, rural roads. The evaluation record is genuinely two-sided: the Bank's own IEG rated the portfolio's outcomes among the strongest in the region while documenting weak sustainability and the Kabul-centric delivery that bypassed the state it was meant to build. August 2021: the Taliban returned; the Bank halted disbursements within days (a portfolio of roughly $1.5 billion frozen mid-project); then the unprecedented carve-out — donors re-purposed the ARTF to pay for basic health and education *inside* Afghanistan through UNICEF, UNDP, WHO, and the World Food Programme (a $1.5 billion-plus reorientation; health facilities kept their lights on; teachers and nurses paid through NGO payrolls) — keeping the population alive while refusing to fund the state. The frozen central-bank reserves (a separate US-led dispute) remain partly locked. The Bank's trusteeship answered the question its Articles leave open: it can be the world's most sophisticated humanitarian channel — precisely when its development function is impossible.

**Iraq (2003– ).** Post-invasion reconstruction ran largely outside the Bank (the Coalition Provisional Authority's $60 billion program, of which the US inspector general estimated some $8 billion wasted or unaccounted). The Bank administered a modest multi-donor trust fund (~$459 million from dozens of donors), cleared Iraq's arrears through the standard 2003–04 Paris Club machinery, and re-engaged as security allowed. The lesson Iraq teaches is comparative: in the worst-governed environments, the Bank is often the *least* corrupt actor in the room — which is precisely its selling point, and precisely the reason its presence is requested.

**Myanmar.** Lending from the 1970s (infrastructure and agriculture through the socialist era), interruption around the 1988 coup, a mid-1990s re-engagement, suspension amid arrears, clearance in late 2012 (a $700 million bridge-financing sequence: Norway, Japan, and the ADB covering the MDB and IMF shares), then a full re-engagement that financed the country's opening — cut by roughly half after the 2017 Rohingya expulsion and frozen entirely after the February 2021 coup (a lending pause; the Bank's Yangon office closed; trust-fund pivoting to community COVID response through NGOs). The engagement calendar maps human-rights politics with a lag of years — the structural rhythm of Article IV §10.

**Ethiopia.** The Protection of Basic Services (PBS) program — budget support channeled through local governments, scaled to over a billion dollars a year at its height — was both the delivery triumph and the accountability scandal of the 2000s. The triumph: the Bank's own evaluations and independent studies found under-five mortality halved in program areas and primary enrollment up by double digits; Ethiopia became the IDA showcase. The scandal: the same congressional testimony that exposed Kenya's Arid Lands documented parallel supervision failures in Ethiopia; when the Tigray war came (November 2020 – November 2022; civilian deaths estimated between 300,000 and 800,000), safety-net transfers to more than 335,000 beneficiaries were disrupted and the Bank's damage-assessment partnership with the government (2022) proceeded amid an information blackout; US aid cuts (March 2025) later hollowed out the humanitarian layer the Bank's programs depended on. Both the Bank's defenders and its prosecutors find their best evidence in Ethiopia.

**Sudan and the arrears-clearance machine.** The 2021 clearance sequence is the template, documented end to end: December 2020 — the US announces a $1.15 billion bridge loan to clear Sudan's IDA arrears (its share of roughly $2.5 billion owed to multilaterals, alongside AfDB $657 million–$413 million and EIB $418 million); January 2021 — a Memorandum of Understanding; February 24, 2021 — the Sudan Family Support Program launches (a $410 million IDA cash-transfer program plus a $410 million multi-donor trust fund); March 25–26, 2021 — the Bank approves a $1.365 billion Re-engagement and Reform Development Policy Financing (the first new Bank lending to Sudan in three decades; 84 percent of it repays the bridge); June 29, 2021 — the IMF clears Sudan's arrears ($1.39 billion) with a French bridge, and Sudan reaches the HIPC decision point (the 38th country; external debt $56.2 billion in net-present-value terms, 163 percent of GDP; arrears $51.9 billion; relief promised of $23.3 billion NPV, including $23.75 billion nominal from Paris Club creditors — France, Austria, the US, Belgium, and Italy the largest). May 2021 — the Paris conference (Macron) celebrates. Within two years: war (April 15, 2023; the Sudanese Armed Forces–Rapid Support Forces war), a portfolio frozen mid-disbursement, and a new emergency architecture (a Catastrophe Containment window; third-party implementation through UNDP and the World Food Programme under the eight-year IDA–UNDP partnership). The machine clears arrears; it does not build states. Sudan's cycle — 1994 arrears, 2021 clearance, 2023 war — is the machine's parable.

**Zimbabwe.** In nonaccrual since October 2000 (its last IBRD loan 1994; last IDA financing 1998), owing about $1.5 billion to the Bank (of roughly $2.5 billion owed to multilaterals) — a quarter-century zombie debt distorting every portfolio statistic the Bank publishes. Token payments (~$50 million in 2024, ~$20 million-plus in prior years) keep the file technically alive; arrears-clearance plans (a structured dialogue process since 2023) await a political settlement the Bank cannot create and the government will not finance. China is Zimbabwe's largest bilateral creditor (~$2 billion). The preferred-creditor paradox in its purest form: the Bank can neither forgive (its Articles and its credit model bar it) nor collect; it can only wait, and lend to Zimbabwe's neighbors instead.

**The Common Framework era (Chad, Zambia, Ghana, Ethiopia).** The G20's Common Framework (November 2020) produced its first completed cases in the mid-2020s after years of negotiation: Chad (agreement November 2022 — China's debut as an official-creditor co-chair, France the Paris Club co-chair, Glencore at the commercial table — with the state-contingent repayment triggers its legacy); Zambia (default 2020; official-creditor memorandum October 2023; bondholder exchange June 2024 — roughly $840 million of relief foregone by bondholders; the Chinese banks (ICBC, China Development Bank) signing accords in September 2024; by end-October 2024, 88 percent of claims resolved); Ghana (completed within the sequence); Ethiopia (pending as of this writing). The Bank's structural role: its preferred-creditor status requires it to keep net flows positive as a condition of everyone else's relief — meaning the Bank lends into distress *before* private creditors share pain, a rule private creditors know and price. Sri Lanka (2022–24) showed the middle-income gap: outside the Framework, its restructuring leaned on an Indian bridge (~$4 billion in January–April 2022 facilities — a $400 million RBI swap, a $500 million Asian Clearing Union deferral, a $500 million fuel credit line, a $1 billion State Bank of India line — which kept $900 million of multilateral payments current and was exempted from restructuring), a Bank emergency package (about $600 million in April–June 2022 for essential payments, plus a later $712 million conversion of eleven IBRD/IDA loans from floating to fixed rates saving some $38 million in present value), electricity prices up 140 percent, and 500,000 households disconnected. The Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable (launched 2023) maintains the negotiation table; the system's own evaluators concede the process remains too slow to be a safety net — Zambia took four years from default to completion.

**Greece (2010–2015) and the troika lesson.** The Bank was a bit player in the euro crisis — no lending, a technical-assistance role — but the IMF's own Independent Evaluation Office documented the governing fact of the era: when the IMF staff proposed, in November 2010, haircuts for Irish senior unsecured bank bondholders, **the US Treasury Secretary vetoed the proposal on a G7 teleconference** — the Fund's court of appeal was the G7, not its board, and the ruling favored European and American banks. The systemic-exemption clause that bent the IMF's own exceptional-access rules for Greece in May 2010 was adopted by the board without the eurogroup even being told; Executive Directors were systematically under-informed on debt sustainability; the "troika" of the time (IMF, European Commission, European Central Bank, with the Bank in advisory attendance) fielded thirty-to-forty-person missions whose information asymmetries the IEO catalogued. The lesson generalizes to the whole Bretton Woods system: in a crisis, the largest shareholders govern by telephone.

**Chad, again.** HIPC completion point April 2015; within months, the Glencore prepayment structure had re-leveraged the state; the 2018 oil-price-triggered restructuring; the Bank's 2008 exit from the pipeline's revenue-management framework (Part V's Chad-Cameroon entry, §21-adjacent history) a decade before had already taught what Chad's creditors relearned in 2015–18. The resource-curse cycle the Bank was created in part to break, intact. (The full pipeline story: the 2000 approval of the Chad–Cameroon Petroleum Development and Pipeline Project — $3.7 billion, the largest private-sector infrastructure project in Africa at the time, a consortium of ExxonMobil (operator), Petronas, and Chevron; the Bank's structural adjustment and IFC loans totaling about $190 million bought a revenue-management law: 10 percent of royalties to a future-generations fund, 80 percent of the rest to priority sectors, monitored by an oversight committee with parliamentarians and civil society and an escrow account in London. The law was amended in December 2005 — security spending reclassified as priority spending, the future-generations account raided; in January 2006 the Bank suspended the program (Wolfowitz's call; the $124 million IDA credit frozen); a 2006 interim agreement; in August 2006 Chad expelled Chevron and Petronas from the consortium (accusing them of unpaid taxes), settled for $289 million; on September 9, 2008 the Bank formally withdrew from the pipeline's revenue-management role; Chad prepaid its remaining loan balance ($65.7 million). The Bank's own exit statement conceded the experiment had failed. Deby died in April 2021, on election day, the day before his fifth-term inauguration was to be certified; his son rules now.)

**The West Bank and Gaza.** The Bank has been the Palestinian Authority's fiscal auditor and the territory's economic weatherman for two decades — its "Economic Monitoring Report" and the IMF's "Macrocritical" updates the only independent accounts of the Palestinian economy. The documented strangulation: since October 2023 the clearance-revenue system (the Paris Protocol mechanism under which Israel collects Palestinian taxes and remits them) has been weaponized — Israeli sanctions on Palestinian banks processing the money, indemnity letters demanded as lawsuit protection after a terrorism-liability judgment against the PA (the Smotrich threats of March 2024 and June 2025; the sanctions response; the two-month extensions through December 2025 and February 2026), correspondent-banking cutoff threats (Israeli banks severing ties; the Palestinian banks' liquidity drying), tens of billions of shekels of Palestinian tax revenue withheld, and an excess-cash trap estimated at over 23 billion shekels. The damage ledger (the Bank's own, with the EU and UN): the Gaza war's destruction priced in the Bank's rapid assessments in the billions; 745,000 students out of school at the count; nearly half the hospitals non-operational. The Bank's own statement — that no operational activities or financial transfers to Gaza are foreseen, and re-engagement would occur only if conditions allow — marks the limit of what the institution will do when politics closes the door. And it is the immediate backdrop of the Board of Peace crisis: the same territory, a different instrument, the Bank as limited trustee (Part VII).

**Apartheid-era South Africa, in full.** The Bank lent to the apartheid state from the 1950s — roads, power, railways — its last loan an electricity utility credit in 1966, after African and American leaders (Albert Luthuli; Martin Luther King) had called for economic sanctions. The Bank's defense of the era was the doctrine of the time: apartheid was a political matter, and Article IV §10 barred political judgments. The re-engagement awaited Mandela's signal (1990–91; president Preston presiding; the first post-apartheid loans in 1993). The record, like the Aswan record, shows the Bank following the political weather of its owners — not leading it.

---
**India — the largest customer.** No country uses the Bank more. The relationship runs from the first loans of the 1950s (community development, the Damodar Valley) through the Green Revolution-era lending to the present: India graduated from IDA in fiscal 2014 (crossing the income threshold) and became the IBRD's single largest borrower — roughly $24.4 billion of outstanding debt by August 2026, the largest country exposure in the institution's book, held under a single-country borrowing limit of $18.5 billion (the exception tier created in 2018; China and Mexico sit at $17 billion, Brazil at $16.5 billion). The current program, the FY2026–31 Country Partnership Framework (announced January 30, 2026): IBRD financing of $20 billion across 79 operations; IFC of $16.72 billion across 174 projects; MIGA guarantees of $618 million; total World Bank Group engagement of $8–10 billion a year. Named current operations: a $830 million skills operation (PM-SETU, training a million young people in industrial training institutes); $490 million for resilient agriculture in Maharashtra; $280 million for Kerala's health system; $750 million through Credila for education financing (190,000 students) — plus the electric-vehicle payment-security mechanism. The arc: record annual lending of $5.1 billion in FY2020; the Doing Business years (India's ranking campaign, 142nd to 63rd between 2014 and 2020 — built substantially on the methodological changes later discredited in the scandal file, a fact no Indian government mentions); the Narmada legacy (Part V §21); and the present partnership, in which the Bank finances India's infrastructure while India's contractors win contracts across Africa (the Chinese-contractor pattern's mirror). The UK's International Climate Finance provided a $1 billion guarantee enabling Bank climate lending to India at scale. The British aid watchdog's review of the UK-financed program (the ICAI) found a $22.8 billion portfolio across 92 operations — effective but audit-deficient in climate tagging. India is where the institution's future volume lives; it is also, through the double-majority debate, a claimant to the institution's future governance.

**Pakistan — the flood ledger.** The 2022 monsoon floods (33 million people affected; 1,739–1,760 recorded deaths) produced the era's largest climate-disaster assessment: the Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (Government of Pakistan, the Asian Development Bank, the EU, the UN, and the Bank) priced damage at $14.9 billion, economic loss at $15.2 billion, and reconstruction needs at $16.3 billion (October 28, 2022); the government's own climate-council assessment ran higher ($40 billion damage, $30.1 billion losses). The Geneva pledging conference (January 2023) raised $10.57 billion — the Islamic Development Bank $4.2 billion, the Bank $2 billion (later $2.7 billion), the ADB $1.5 billion, the AIIB $1 billion, Saudi Arabia $1 billion, France $345 million, China $100 million, the United States $100 million, the EU $93 million, Germany $88 million, Japan $77 million. The Bank's own pledge, decomposed: $650 million of repurposed projects, $1.3 billion from Pakistan's IDA allocation (7 percent of the country envelope), and $700 million from the Crisis Response Window — about 90 percent of it loans; tracking found only about 30 percent of the pledged international total had arrived. The immediate response: $370 million of relief within weeks plus $2 billion of portfolio reallocation; the Benazir Income Support Program's cash transfers (37.2 billion Pakistani rupees to 1.5 million families); the Sindh resilience follow-ons. Later: a $1 billion climate-resilience development policy loan (2024) — Pakistan being where the Bank's climate lending, the pledge-gap pattern, and the IMF program cycle (the 2023 standby and its successors) all meet.

**Türkiye — the earthquake response.** The February 6, 2023 earthquakes (more than 50,000 dead; 3.3 million displaced; over 800,000 buildings damaged across eleven provinces and fourteen million people) produced the Bank's fastest large-scale deployment: a Global Rapid Assessment of Damage and Loss (GRADE) within three weeks ($34.2 billion of direct damages; a follow-on government-EU-UNDP-Bank needs assessment at $81.5 billion, presented at the Brussels donors' conference of March 20, 2023); an immediate $1.78 billion package in the first week (contingent emergency response components of $780 million activated from two existing projects — the Turkey Emergency Flood and Earthquake Recovery Project and the Climate Resilient Cities Project — plus $1 billion of new financing); and on June 27, 2023, the Board-approved Turkey Earthquake Recovery and Reconstruction Project ($1 billion; the municipal bank ILBANK as implementing agency for municipal infrastructure and health facilities; the environment ministry for rural housing; the Debt Law 4749 amended March 15, 2023 to allow grants to ILBANK; condensed procurement under emergency procedures; a $1 million Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery technical-assistance grant from the US Agency for International Development (USAID)). The longer tail: Additional Financing 1 (March 2025, €200 million/$207.5 million, matched by France's AFD at €200 million — 2,800 homes for 9,000 people) and Additional Financing 2 (July 2, 2026 — housing for a further 10,470 people, the housing target doubled from 7,000 to 16,000). Named along the way: Anna Bjerde (vice president for the region, later managing director), Humberto Lopez (country director), Bontje Marie Zaengerling (project team leader); AFAD (the disaster agency) and ILBANK (the lender) as counterparts; GFDRR as the analytics engine. The episode is the Bank's crisis-playbook at its best: pre-arranged components, rapid assessment, municipal-finance delivery.

**Morocco — the parametric proof.** The September 8, 2023 Al Haouz earthquake (magnitude 6.9; roughly 2,960–3,000 dead; 60,000 buildings; more than 300,000 affected) tested the disaster-risk-financing stack the Bank had helped Morocco build since the 2004 Al Hoceima quake: technical assistance (2008–13), the Solidarity Fund (FSEC) capitalized by a parafiscal tax on non-life insurance premiums (about $25 million a year; $90 million accumulated by 2020–23), and a sovereign risk-transfer program. The October 2023 natural-disaster declaration unlocked roughly $300 million within weeks ($275 million from the sovereign reinsurance policy renewed in 2023, plus reserves and private-insurance recoveries) — against a Bank-estimated $3 billion of direct damage (2.6 percent of GDP). The Bank's own operations layered a catastrophe-deferred-drawdown policy loan and emergency response financing; Bank-supported work on a flood-parametric cover and a catastrophe bond feasibility study continues (the Marrakesh Annual Meetings, October 2023, convened in the aftershock). Named: Reda Aboutadjine and John Plevin of the Bank's disaster-analytics practice. The lesson the system absorbed: the countries that pre-arrange financing recover faster — and the Bank's job is making pre-arrangement the default (the Cat DDO and disaster-clause agenda of Part II §8.3).

**Lebanon — lending into collapse.** A state in sovereign default since 2020, without a president for extended periods, with a banking system destroyed: the Bank's re-engagement ran through targeted energy and water operations. October 3, 2024: a $250 million grid-and-renewables project (the national utility EDL and the Litani River Authority; $90 million of solar generation gated on the creation of an electricity regulatory authority); signed April 24, 2025 (Finance Minister Yassine Jaber; the Bank's Jean-Christophe Carret for the region); June 2025: the Lebanon Emergency Assistance Program (LEAP, $250 million, scalable within a $1 billion framework — a rapid damage and needs assessment of $7.2 billion damage against $11 billion of needs, the Council for Development and Reconstruction implementing). The Bank's role in Lebanon is the humanitarian-development nexus in practice: no macroeconomic program (the IMF's remains unratified by the political system), no budget support — but lights, water, and eventually food security kept on life support through ring-fenced operations.

**Argentina — the serial program, again.** Milei's Argentina and the Bank's 2024–26 engagement, in full: December 2023 — the Latin American development bank CAF provides a $960 million bridge; December 2024 — an IDB–World Bank package of about $4 billion (the Bank's share roughly $3.5 billion); April 2025 — the Bank approves $1.5 billion of new financing; April 15, 2025 — the IMF approves a $20 billion extended fund facility (the twenty-third Argentine program since 1958; $12 billion disbursed immediately, further tranches through the year); January 2026 — the US Treasury opens a $20 billion exchange-stabilization-fund currency swap (only $2.5 billion drawn by late October 2025, the facility's terms kept secret and the congressional scrutiny ongoing); 2026 — with July maturities of about $4.3 billion looming, the Bank arranges a guarantee of up to $2 billion (IBRD plus MIGA) alongside a $550 million IDB guarantee to refinance the wall of debt — the Caputo–Banga meeting in April 2026 sealing the package, the IDB accelerating $7.2 billion of its program. The social ledger: poverty above 30 percent on official counts; inflation re-accelerating through mid-2026; the government's approval near 36 percent. Argentina is the recurring demonstration that the Bank-IMF system lends to the same fifteen countries on the same thirty-day cycles for seventy years — and that guarantees, not loans, are now the instrument that keeps the music playing.

**Egypt — the megaproject swap.** March 2024: the Bank announces a three-year program of $6 billion-plus (half government programs, half private sector; the first $500 million disbursing within months) alongside an IMF extended facility expanded to $8 billion (plus a $1.3 billion resilience-and-sustainability loan); the same season, the Ras El-Hekma deal converts the UAE's Abu Dhabi Developmental Holding commitment into $35 billion of Egypt relief ($24 billion of new foreign-exchange inflows plus the conversion of $11 billion of existing deposits) in exchange for prime Mediterranean coastline; the currency floats in March 2024 (the pound losing half its value); S&P upgrades Egypt to B in October 2025 — the first upgrade in seven years — and an IMF combined-review disbursement of $2.5 billion plus the Bank's share flows before year-end 2025. External debt stands around $161 billion. The pattern: the Bank's programmatic money, the IMF's anchor, and Gulf sovereign capital trade in the same window — development finance as a package deal with real estate at the margin.

**Liberia and Sierra Leone — the war economies.** The Bank's West African war files, in full. Liberia: Charles Taylor — elected 1997 after the civil war he had launched, convicted April 26, 2012 by the Special Court for Sierra Leone (the first head of state before an international court since Nuremberg; eleven counts: terror, murder, rape, sexual slavery, use of child soldiers, enslavement, mutilation, pillage; fifty years; he died in 2022) — financed his war through timber and diamonds; the Bank's own post-conflict window lent into the transition (the Emergency Post-Conflict Assistance credit of 2006, after the arrears clearance: the UK's DFID bridge-loaned the arrears payment, repaid by the Bank credit — the original template of the machine later applied to Sudan). Global Witness's *Undue Diligence* (2009) traced how Citibank's correspondent banking for the Liberian banking system moved Taylor's money while he was under UN sanctions — the global-bank counterpart to the trader lane. The DLH case in France (Global Witness, Sherpa, and Greenpeace, "Bankrolling Brutality," 2005–09) attempted corporate liability for a Swiss timber trader's facilitation of arms-for-timber; the courts ultimately declined — the accountability gap the OECD guidelines later tried to close. Sierra Leone: the conflict-diamond decade (the RUF's campaign; the 2006 HIPC completion point); the 2014–16 Ebola response in which the Bank committed $1.62 billion to the three affected countries ($450 million of emergency response; $105 million rapid response; $600 million of IFC commercial financing) — the moment the institution learned epidemic response. The aid-system footnote that belongs here: the UK Parliament's 2014 review of DFID's Sierra Leone and Liberia programs found the department paying fund managers — including PwC — to run recovery programs, the consultancy-shadow pattern inside the aid system at large.

**Yemen — the trustee-of-a-failed-state.** The Arab world's poorest country before its war; the Bank's portfolio became the state's last functional interface. The documented architecture: an active portfolio of about $2 billion across nine projects; the Yemen Resilience, Recovery and Reconstruction Trust Fund (UK, Netherlands, Switzerland co-financing) and an EU-IFC private-sector window; the eight-year IDA–UNDP partnership (December 2024) whose programs (YECRP, ESPECRP, FSRRP, SFISH — executed through the Social Fund for Development, the Public Works Project, and SMEPS) have delivered over half a million jobs and income opportunities, food security for 6.5 million people, 843 kilometers of roads, 2,552 classrooms, and 36,910 hectares of irrigated land; third-party implementation throughout (UNDP, UNICEF, the World Food Programme) since the 2021 collapse of state counterparts. June 4, 2026: the Bank launches its new Country Engagement Note with four operations ($94 million for health, nutrition, and water; $153.6 million for water and irrigation under a multiphase program; $21 million of additional urban-services financing; $20 million for governance) alongside $30 million of grant agreements (UNDP financial-market infrastructure; UNICEF education) signed June 17, 2025. The humanitarian ledger the Bank publishes: about 60 percent of households food-insecure; the program's own targets run water access from 3.4 million people toward 6.4 million by 2030. Named: Khawja Aftab Ahmed of the IFC for the Levant–Iraq–Yemen portfolio. Yemen is what the trustee identity looks like when the state it is meant to serve has ceased to exist.

# PART VI — THE PATTERNS

Across eighty years and hundreds of cases, seven patterns recur. They are the institution's deep grammar — the reason individual reforms keep failing, and the map of what any real fix must change. Each pattern below is stated, then evidenced at full length.

## 25. Pattern One — The Revolving Door

*The claim:* the institution's leadership circulates between Wall Street, government, and the Bank; the perspective of finance is structurally over-represented; and the boundary between the Bank and the capital it is meant to mobilize has dissolved into formal advisory structures.

*The evidence:* eight of thirteen presidents arrived directly from finance or industry — Eugene Meyer (Lazard Frères, the Washington Post), Eugene Black and John McCloy (Chase), George Woods (First Boston), Tom Clausen (Bank of America), Lew Preston (J.P. Morgan), Jim Wolfensohn (Salomon Brothers, his own investment bank), Bob Zoellick (Goldman Sachs vice chairman), Ajay Banga (Mastercard, General Atlantic); the others from the Pentagon (McNamara from Ford and the Defense Department; Wolfowitz as deputy defense secretary), Congress (Conable), the Treasury (Malpass), and medicine (Kim). The traffic runs both ways: Zoellick returned to Goldman-adjacent advisory boards; Kim departed to a private infrastructure firm absorbed into BlackRock; Banga came from the payments industry the Bank now champions as development finance. The institutionalization: the Private Sector Investment Lab (launched 2023 under IFC, chaired by Shriti Vadera, former Prudential vice-chair), whose membership reads as the mobilization target's own board — BlackRock, Temasek, AXA, HSBC, Macquarie, Standard Chartered, MUFG, Ping An, Tata Sons, Temasek — expanded in 2025 to Bayer, Bharti, Dangote, and Hyatt; its first product was an IFC-arranged $510 million CLO for emerging-market lenders. The investors the Bank exists to mobilize are writing the mobilization strategy. No illegality; a structure of captured perspective. The same pattern runs down the hierarchy: IFC's senior ranks and the AMC fund managers circulate through the private-equity industry the Bank evaluates; the staff's own retirement money (~$20–50 billion) is managed in-house by the same treasury that prices the deals. The 2026 succession debate — "the next president must be a woman and cannot be an American" — is, among other things, an argument against the door.

## 26. Pattern Two — Rating Inflation

*The claim:* the institution's own measures of its success have drifted away from the underlying reality, because measuring well is rewarded and delivering well is hard; the drift is structural, documented across three decades, and periodically rediscovered.

*The evidence:* the base rate is the Wapenhans report (1992): about 11 percent of the ongoing portfolio in major trouble (FY1981) had become 20 percent (FY1991), with 37.5 percent of a broader retrospective sample unsatisfactory, and an "approval culture" — the pressure to lend rewarded at entry, punished for delay — as the mechanism. The modern rate: 76 percent of projects rated satisfactory-or-above in FY2016 rising to 84 percent by FY2021 (IEG's Results and Performance report) — while, in the same report's own words, only four of sixteen outcome areas actually improved, "all of them areas where the Bank had simply become better at measuring." The Bank's own internal auditors (2015) found that problem projects carrying high monitoring-and-evaluation ratings received satisfactory outcome ratings 85 percent of the time, versus 45 percent for identical projects with low M&E ratings — the rating follows the paperwork, not the project. The IEG's 2024 report: more than a third of operations still have inadequate M&E; 83 percent of country programs had major results-framework inadequacies; MIGA behind on its self-evaluations; IFC's own dashboard indicators showing higher achievement than IEG-validated results. The institutional memory: the Quality Assurance Group created after Wapenhans was disbanded in 2010 (a cost-saving); the management-action record on evaluation recommendations shows management claiming 100 percent implementation where IEG scores 60–75 percent; and the IEG's December 2025 verdict on the current reorganization repeats the 2014 failure notes nearly verbatim. The type specimen remains Kenya's Arid Lands: 62 percent of 28,000 transactions suspect, fourteen consecutive years of "satisfactory," an "exemplary" rating in 2007. When the institution's thermometer reads what the institution wishes, every fever is invisible until it is fatal.

## 27. Pattern Three — The Parallel Architecture

*The claim:* when borrowers' systems fail fiduciary tests, the Bank routes money around them — trust funds, FIFs, emergency vehicles — and the exception has become the architecture; the bypass solves the accountability problem by deleting the sovereign from its own development, with measurable costs.

*The evidence:* Haiti, controlled experiment: 99 percent of relief and 86 percent of recovery money bypassed Haitian public systems; under 7 percent of bilateral recovery funds through the government; less than one percent of humanitarian funding reaching Haitian organizations; the Interim Haiti Recovery Commission expiring unrenewed. Afghanistan: the ARTF's NGO/UN channel after 2021 — the population served, the state deleted. Ukraine: PEACE and FORTIS move budget support as grants under donor committees, not borrower's parliaments. Gaza: GRAD with the Bank's fiduciary duty contractually disarmed. The structural inventory: 748 trust funds at peak; two dozen FIFs holding tens of billions; the Bank's trustee business (GEF, GCF, Global Fund/IFFIm, Pandemic Fund, Loss and Damage) making it the world's fiscal agent. The costs, quantified: loan commitments miss disbursement three times as often as grants ($54 billion versus $17.7 billion in the 2005–24 African ledger); IDA's own disbursement lag (5.5 years average; a third undelivered at five years) is the worst among multilaterals; Bangladesh's $8 billion of approved-but-undisbursed projects (11.8 months average to first disbursement; extensions "without reasons") shows the machine failing at its own objective. And the deepest cost is sovereign: thirty years of capacity-building rhetoric versus a system that, when governance is weakest, most completely removes the state — the very condition under which states never learn.

## 28. Pattern Four — The Pledge-Announcement Gap

*The claim:* in development finance, the announcement is a product with its own market; delivery is under-measured, under-enforced, and chronically late; and the gap falls hardest on the poorest, who cannot collateralize promises.

*The evidence:* Haiti: $10.4 billion pledged; 51 percent of recovery money delivered in three years; the UN's own tracker (OSE) documenting per-donor shortfalls. Gleneagles 2005 (the G8's $50 billion promise): the delivery arithmetic, tracked by the development NGOs for a decade, fell chronically short and was never enforced by any mechanism. Gavi, June 2025 (Brussels pledging summit): $9 billion-plus announced against an $11.9 billion target — with the headline inflated by counting COVAX leftovers, refinancing, and future payment promises (Norway's "8 billion Norwegian kroner" containing only NOK 5 billion new and upfront, down from 6 billion before; Australia's A$386 million including IFFIm payments; the US contribution zeroed). The Loss and Damage Fund: roughly $730 million pledged (the US $17.5 million, Japan $10 million, against France, Italy, Germany, and the UAE's hundreds of millions); the would-be beneficiaries' "empty shell" verdict; the Bank's 20.4 percent cost-recovery charge on its own hosting costs. IDA: the $100 billion announced against the $120 billion African ask; the history of the flat donor line ($23–25 billion per cycle for two decades) behind ever-larger headline envelopes. The 2005–24 African ledger: $71.7 billion promised-and-not-delivered; loan gaps three times grant gaps. The mechanism is incentive-shaped: announcements are costless and credit-bearing; disbursement is costly and audited; no forum publishes a running, binding pledge-delivery ledger except the UN's own small OSE unit, which was wound down. Announcement is the product; delivery is somebody else's problem.

## 29. Pattern Five — The Crisis Ratchet

*The claim:* each emergency permanently enlarges the mandate; nothing is ever sunset; and the institution's growth is crisis-driven, not strategy-driven.

*The evidence:* the 1982 debt crisis produced structural adjustment — which became a permanent product line (dozens of countries at a time for two decades). The 1997 Asian crisis produced the governance agenda — now a Global Practice. The 2008 crisis produced the crisis-finance apparatus (the Vulnerability Financing Facility and countercyclical policy loans) and a $100 billion surge. COVID produced the vaccine envelope ($12 billion scaled to $20 billion), the Pandemic Fund, and a $157–204 billion surge. Ukraine produced war-risk insurance, a new trust-fund-plus-guarantee architecture, and FORTIS. Gaza produced GRAD and the Board of Peace. Each layer was justified by its emergency, and none was ever closed; the institution's strategy documents (the 2022 Evolution Roadmap most explicitly) now formalize the ratchet — global public goods bolted onto the poverty mission with "selectivity" as the only brake. The FY2025 portfolio tells the aggregate: $161.9 billion committed across every sector and every arm — the anti-poverty bank now financing everything, everywhere, at record scale, with a scorecard of 22 indicators trying to hold the line. The Meltzer Commission's 2000 answer to ratchet was abolition ("irrelevant to its poverty mission... a 65–70 percent failure rate in the poorest countries"); the institution's answer is always another mandate; and the borrowers — whose crises set the ratchet — get no vote on either.

## 30. Pattern Six — Reform Amnesia

*The claim:* every scandal produces a reform; every reform is quietly unwound by the volume imperative; and the institution possesses no working mechanism for remembering failure.

*The evidence:* the cycle ledger. Wapenhans (1992) → the Quality Assurance Group → disbanded 2010. The consultant-trust-fund scandal (1999) → the Integrity Vice Presidency → its Volcker-era independence eroded (the Thornburgh and Volcker reviews' own recommendations — board-level oversight, protection for staff who report — half-implemented). Wolfowitz's expulsion (2007) → an ethics reformation → the Chaves case shows a decade of known misconduct managed to demotion. Doing Business (2021) → B-READY with the ranking removed but the country-competitive data product rebuilt. The 2014 matrix reorganization → "since disbanded" (the IEG's own 2025 note). The harassment Action Plan (2019) → the Banga-era apology with no accountability attached. The Zedillo Commission (2009): twenty board seats, all elected chairs, the US veto cut to 80 percent, the IMF-quota link severed, a non-American president possible — sixteen years later, none of it exists, and the 2025 review produced "modest measures." The evaluation function's December 2025 verdict: "no formal system for learning from canceled or dropped operations and from failure." Voice reform: −5 percentage points (2008–10), +0.5 (2018), zero (2020). The amnesia is not accidental: it is the immune system of Pattern Two. Institutions that forget their failures are doomed to re-lend them.

## 31. Pattern Seven — Shareholder Capture

*The claim:* when the largest shareholder's geopolitics engage, the institution's rules bend — and the bending has escalated from agenda-setting (Aswan, 1956) to rule-bending (the Irish-bondholder veto, 2010) to target-removal (climate, 2026) to personal participation (the Board of Peace, 2026).

*The evidence:* the ladder. Aswan (1956): the Bank's project killed by a State Department decision taken over its president's objection; the Bank lending Egypt canal money within four years anyway. The 1991–2000 Iran lending blockade and its G7-managed breach: the Bank's lending program subordinated to a foreign-policy coalition it was not part of. Iraq (2003): the Bank's re-engagement held until Washington's occupation needed international cover. The 2010 euro crisis: the US Treasury vetoing IMF staff positions on a G7 call; the Fund's systemic exemption adopted without the Europeans informed. The 2012–16 gentleman's-agreement defenses: the American nominee process insulated from merit competition by the 85 percent lock. Doing Business: the data altered under pressure that arrived, per the WilmerHale report, partly as a favor to the shareholder whose support the capital increase needed. The 2024–26 climate sequence: the 45 percent target announced under shareholder pressure (2023), exceeded (2025), and retired (2026) when the shareholder changed its mind; the nineteen-director counter-letter unable to prevail. And the capstone — the Board of Peace: the sitting president's personal seat on the shareholder's political instrument, the institution's fiduciary duty contractually disarmed ("no responsibility or accountability, fiduciary or otherwise, for the use of funds after transfer"), the trustee agreement unpublished, $1.25 billion of reprogrammed US appropriations as the first money, and the Articles' political clauses (IV §10, V §5) engaged as never before. The defenders' case is also on the record: the Bank salvaged a multilateral channel into a catastrophe the UN system could not fund, kept the money flowing where no one else would, and preserved a seat — however compromised — at the table. Both things are true, which is the condition the institution has always lived in, and which this document has now traced from July 1944 to the present.

# PART VII — THE CURRENT STATE (2025–2026)

## 32. The Reform President and the Second Reform

Banga's first two years produced real change, documented: the **Evolution Roadmap** (endorsed October 2023 at the Marrakesh Annual Meetings) rewrote the mission — "ending poverty on a livable planet" — and the accompanying documents walked the institution through a genuine doctrinal shift: global public goods integrated into the country model; a new **Corporate Scorecard** (Part II §11); streamlined operations (preparation times cut from nineteen to twelve months; total strategic policy commitments across IDA21 cut to twenty-five); a programmatic approach to country engagement; the job-creation strategic theme (the April 2025 Development Committee communiqué carried "Jobs: The Path to Prosperity"); and the money architecture of IDA21 — a $100 billion envelope for FY2026–28, built on donor pledges of $23.7 billion (announced in South Korea, December 5–6, 2024; the resolution approved April 25, 2025), with five Focus Areas (People; Prosperity; Planet; Infrastructure; Digital Development), four Lenses (gender, jobs, private investment, FCV) cutting across everything, the **GROW window** (financing global and regional challenges — $15.9 billion, expanded so countries no longer sacrifice base allocations to global goods), a Crisis Response Window of $3.7 billion, the Small Island and Economies Window, the Private Sector Window at $3.2 billion (including $500 million of IFC economic capital), and the new leverage instruments (hybrid capital; the portfolio-guarantee platform). The Bank's own scorecard at mid-2025: climate co-benefits at 48–49 percent; disbursement records; and — the reform's critics note — the grant share down to about 9 percent of IDA (the Bretton Woods Project's D-grade), the GROW window's non-PBA money weakening the performance-allocation discipline, and the $100 billion below both the African ask ($120 billion) and the tripling arithmetic ($279 billion).

## 33. April 2026: The Spring Meetings Rupture

The 113th Development Committee meeting (April 16, 2026) produced **no communiqué and no chair's statement — the first such failure in the committee's history**. The full map of the positions, as recorded: the United States (Treasury Secretary Bessent at the table) demanded the "long-overdue expiration" of the Climate Change Action Plan, the immediate removal of the 45 percent climate target ("distortionary," a "rigid requirement" diverting resources "away from the core mission"), an all-of-the-above energy posture explicitly including gas, oil, and coal, and the de-prioritization of global public goods; the European Union defended the status quo; Brazil spoke for a group of nine countries demanding counter-cyclical tools, lower spreads, waived front-end fees, grace periods, and a multiphase food-security financing strategy; the United Kingdom (Baroness Chapman) pushed early crisis-toolkit deployment, contingent debt agreements, a Common Framework extended to middle-income countries, pause clauses, and the London sustainable-sovereign-debt coalition; Japan pressed quality-infrastructure procurement standards, debt-data reconciliation (the DSE playbook, Indonesia's real-time digital reconciliation), and its own crisis contributions (a Solomon Islands yen loan with a disaster clause — a first); Germany framed renewables as a strategic imperative in a war-disrupted energy market (the Strait of Hormuz crisis was live); the ILO's director-general (Houngbo) warned on workers' rights; Argentina's deregulation minister was given a podium; and a Bank vice president told the region the Bank "does not believe in industrial policy" — hours after a Bank report argued the opposite. Chair Svantesson (Sweden) could not bridge it, and said nothing. Two months later (June 29, 2026) the Bank formally retired the 45 percent climate target while extending the action plan indefinitely pending the independent evaluation the board had ordered. The annual meetings scheduled for October 12–18, 2026, in Bangkok — the first in Southeast Asia (Thailand hosting) — will convene in this atmosphere, with flagship reports on jobs ("Small AI, Big Jobs," October 12) and private-capital mobilization (October 14), a Development Committee meeting that must either repair the communiqué tradition or bury it, and a succession clock ticking toward June 2028.

## 34. The Board of Peace and the GRAD Fund

The defining controversy of the period, at full length. **The origin:** after the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire, the UN Security Council adopted **Resolution 2803 (November 12, 2025)** endorsing a US-conceived "Board of Peace" for Gaza; the Bank was approached to serve as trustee of its reconstruction fund, and a Financial Intermediary Fund — the **Fund for Gaza Reconstruction and Development (GRAD)** — was established in November 2025 after what critics call an obscure consultation. **The charter:** signed January 16, 2026, launched at Davos on January 22 — features without precedent in multilateral practice: Donald Trump named personally as inaugural **chairman**, with the charter silent on any term limit (press framing: "chairman for life"); Article 3.2 giving the chairman exclusive authority over subsidiaries; Article 3.3 a self-designated succession mechanism (replacement only by voluntary resignation or unanimous executive-board vote); a $1 billion contribution threshold for permanent membership; the chairman's power to issue binding directives and interpret the charter finally; the word "Gaza" absent from the text; no mention of the Palestinian Authority or Palestinians in the governance; official language English; headquarters negotiable. **The people:** the executive board pairs US officials (Secretary of State Marco Rubio, envoy Steve Witkoff, Jared Kushner) and Tony Blair with **Ajay Banga in his personal capacity**, alongside Apollo CEO Marc Rowan and Robert Gabriel; a Gaza Executive Board adds Nickolay Mladenov, Turkey's Hakan Fidan, Qatar's Yusuf Al-Thawadi, Mazen Rashad of Jordan, the UAE's Reem Al-Hashimy and Lana Nusseibeh, Israel's Eyal Gabay (the Smotreich-faction official later sanctioned by European states), and UN's Sigrid Kaag. **The accessions:** Lukashenko formally joined January 20, 2026; Putin was invited; Canada's invitation (Carney) was revoked after a Davos speech; France publicly refused ("no to creating an organisation which would replace the United Nations"); Norway and the Nordics declined. **The money:** the United States pledged $10 billion; on March 26, 2026, the State Department transferred the first **$1.25 billion of reprogrammed appropriations** — $1 billion from international disaster assistance, $200 million from peacekeeping operations, $50 million from international organizations (Senator Cortez Masto answered with a bill to redirect the disaster money to home-heating assistance (LIHEAP)); a Crisis Group accounting (August 2026) puts the February 2026 pledges at $17 billion, with the Board of Peace itself — not any Palestinian structure — distributing the money, and $400 million of a July 30 roadmap earmarked for a "National Committee for the Administration of Gaza" that began paying salaries only in April 2026, with laptops donated by European governments.

**The Bank's role, precisely:** it serves as **"limited trustee"** — the GRAD documents state the Bank bears **"no responsibility or accountability, fiduciary or otherwise, for the use of funds after transfer"** — a deliberate departure from its standard fiduciary model (the Ukraine, Haiti model), under an agreement the Bank has not published. The legal anatomy, assembled from former insiders' analyses and the CSO record: **Article IV §10** (the political non-interference clause — Banga had written to the US administration endorsing the draft resolution *before* its adoption, per the Reuters report of November 2025; the founding resolution names him personally, not institutionally, so the seat appears to lack Board authorization); **Article V §5** (the presidency is a full-time office; a personal seat on a member-state political instrument is arguably outside its duties); **Article V §8(b)** (the duty to give "due weight" to the ICJ's July 2024 advisory opinion — which found Israel's occupation unlawful and ordered evacuation of settlements — sits unaddressed); **the safeguard framework** dispensed with for the framework operations (no OP 4.12 resettlement analysis, no OP/BP 2.30 conflict assessment); and **the Code of Professional Ethics**. The Rowan conflict compounds it: a Bank that de-risks Gaza reconstruction benefits the investment firm of the board member sitting beside the Bank's president (Apollo's interest in the territory is documented; Rowan has spoken of its coastline in investment terms). **The response record:** Senator Markey's February 19, 2026 letter (Case-Zablocki legal-authority questions; the $1B-forever-membership clause; the Lukashenko and Putin invitations); the eighteen-organization CSO letter (Accountability Counsel and co-signers, February 18, 2026) calling the fund illegitimate, neo-colonial, and geared to real-estate gain; the Palestinian and international coalition letter (April 2026, 180-plus organizations) demanding withdrawal; Al-Haq's April 22, 2026 statement; the protest that interrupted Banga's Atlantic Council speech in April 2026 ("a sham"); Banga's public defense (Reuters, April 7, 2026), which addressed the humanitarian purpose but not the unpublished trustee agreement, the Board authorization question, or the Rowan conflict; and the reform literature's escalation — McIntire's April 2026 analysis ("Why is Ajay Banga still President?"), arguing the dual role is disqualifying, the contract should not be renewed, and the succession must be non-American and female. The Crisis Group's August 2026 verdict frames the stakes: the reconstruction mechanism's transparency lags every comparable Bank fund, and its money is moving through a Board whose disarmament "one authority, one law, one weapon" formula is, as of August 2026, a Kushner ultimatum away from collapse. Whether one reads the episode as the Bank salvaging a multilateral money-flow into a catastrophe no one else would fund, or as the final merger of the institution with its largest shareholder's foreign policy, depends on the prior question this document has built toward: is the Bank a bank, an agency, or an instrument? The Articles say bank; the capture pattern says instrument; the borrowers — who had no seat at the table where the terms were written — would say they know which.

## 35. The Self-Inflicted Crisis Inside

While the external controversies mounted, the 2026 reorganization produced an internal one. The Center for Global Development's diagnosis (April 2026), in full: a centralization replicating the failed 2016 matrix model (which Malpass had wound down); country economists pulled from country offices to the center; a research restructuring seen as weakening quality and independence; an imminent freeze on all short-term-consultant hiring (a quarter of the workforce); a rumored contracting pause to fiscal year-end; IFC–Bank integration repeating a previously failed approach (IFC's tax-advantage and exclusivity concerns surfacing); and the disbanding of the "program leader" roles that anchored country knowledge. The staff survey: only 40 percent see a culture of openness and trust; only half view WBG leadership favorably (direct managers do better at two-thirds); "widespread culture of fear" is the evaluator's phrase. The budget context: a small real decrease in core administrative funding for FY2026 (total resources ~$5.4 to $5.3 billion). The countervailing facts: IFC projects in IDA countries rated satisfactory by IEG only 46 percent of the time (versus 86 percent for IDA's own operations); the red-tape cuts are real (nineteen to twelve months); the equity-to-loans cut is real ($70 billion-plus of headroom); and the FY26 lending momentum is real (IBRD $50.2 billion, up 23 percent). The Bank's defenders read the turmoil as the pain of real change; its staff, as the fourth consecutive reorganization they have been asked to believe in; and the borrowers, who experience the institution through whichever country director survived the reshuffle, as weather.

## 36. The Senegal File

A former Bank country director's forensic reconstruction (2026) — the most serious current-country case, told in full because it shows the machine from the inside. The facts, as documented: Senegal's off-budget borrowing (through the state energy company's commercial paper, undisclosed guarantees, and sukuk proceeds kept off the books) ran at roughly 13 percent of GDP a year by 2023 (813, 392, 785, 1,137, and 1,400 billion CFA francs in the five years); the debt stock was eventually revised from 74.4 percent of GDP (end-2023, as officially reported) to 111.0 percent (the IMF's August 2025 correction) and 118.8 percent (end-2024). The sequence: an IMF stand-by ($650 million, mid-2021 — mission chief Edward Gemayel's team certifying the framework) certifying "moderate risk" while off-budget borrowing already exceeded 15 percent of GDP; a World Bank $300 million IDA policy loan (approved May 16, 2023) whose "adequate macroeconomic framework" certification rested on a June 2022 debt-sustainability analysis — a year that ended with 19 percent of GDP borrowed off-budget; an unusually sequenced IMF package ($1.834 billion — extended fund and credit facilities plus a resilience-and-sustainability trust loan, about 4.5 percent of GDP — June 2023) approved months after the Bank's money rather than before, timed to a domestic political crisis in which President Macky Sall attempted to extend his rule (the June 2023 confrontation; his eventual abandonment of the bid; the April 2, 2024 election of Bassirou Diomaye Faye); a December 2023 Fund review accepting an official "overfinancing" story (a 3.2 percent-of-GDP slush fund explained as cash management); and $1.9 billion of state-guaranteed borrowing (April 2023–February 2024, about 5.4 percent of GDP) that no DSA had reported — all surfaced in the Cour des Comptes' February 2025 audit (errors in amortization schedules, overfinancing, cash-management defects, sukuk balances not returned, incomplete debt accounting, off-budget bank debt, deficits and debt understated). The history it evokes (the author was the Bank's Senegal country director 2000–2004): the Bercy pressure pattern — a French Treasury diktat that cost an operational director his job under Lew Preston (the 1990s); Ambroise Fayolle's "stray bullet" threat to Gobind Nankani (the Bank's Africa vice-president) over Congo-Brazzaville's HIPC candidacy, after which Nankani was fired in 2006; the Camdessus era's French protectorate over francophone Africa's programs; the author's own 2002 run-in with Abdoulaye Wade over a public-private partnership (PPP) law passed "en procédure d'urgence." The remedies proposed, and never taken: an IMF present-value haircut (20 percent) with fee rebates for the years the Fund certified false data; conversion of Bank loans to grants; a halt to budget support (the 2023 Bank loan, he notes, financed the IMF's early repayments); and an Inspection Panel investigation — the accountability path the institution has never taken against itself. The Senegal file connects every pattern in Part VI: the rating machinery certifying fiction (Pattern Two), the parallel money (Pattern Three), the pledge-and-certification gap (Pattern Four), and the shareholder diplomacy that sequences Bank money before Fund money when an ally needs time (Pattern Seven).

## 37. Everything Else on the 2025–26 Desk

- **The nuclear line moved**: the Bank's decades-old exclusion of nuclear financing ended in June 2025 (confirmed in the Cameco annual filing; the AP had reported the internal review; the Bank is exploring IAEA-partnered financing for plant life-extensions and potential new builds in developing countries — the first doctrinal breach of the post-Chernobyl energy consensus).
- **Gavi's shortfall**: the June 25, 2025 Brussels pledging summit (co-hosted by the EU and the Gates Foundation) closed at roughly $9 billion against an $11.9 billion target; the US withdrawal was absolute (a recorded message from the Health Secretary confirming it); the gap arithmetic — 75 million unvaccinated children, projected deaths — is the pledge-gap pattern in live form; the Gates Foundation's $1.6 billion and Team Europe's €2 billion-plus carried the headline.
- **Bangladesh's clog**: the government's own review (the ERD's analysis, amplified by a former Bank Dhaka lead economist) found $8 billion of a $12 billion active portfolio undisbursed, 11.8 months average from approval to first disbursement, effectiveness delays of one to two years, extensions granted "without reasons," and agreements signed via DocuSign before effectiveness; a definitional dispute with the Bank over what "active" means does not change the arithmetic.
- **Malawi's live trial**: the Sattar proceedings continue in London (Part V §23); the Bank's Malawi pipeline remains entangled.
- **The sanctions ledger**: FY2025 closed 19 sanction cases and 19 settlements; FY2026 opened with the six-year debarment of a Moscow rail-signals company (cross-debarred by the AIIB in August 2026) and a steady drumbeat of settlements (Yunnan machinery makers, an Indonesian consultancy, Indian software firms, an Azerbaijani auto dealer, a Nigerian engineer, a Dutch NGO under conditional non-debarment) — the integrity system grinding on, below its pre-pandemic volume.
- **The integration**: January 1, 2026, saw treasury, risk, controls, and environment-social functions consolidated across IBRD, IDA, and IFC under single chiefs (one CFO, one treasurer, one chief risk officer) — the "one Bank" bet that the reorganization critiques target, and the plumbing behind the record FY26 mobilization.
- **Mission 300**: the Africa electrification drive (launched April 2024 with the African Development Bank; the Bank's share targeting 250 million of the 300 million Africans without power by 2030; twelve Electrification Compacts signed at the January 2025 Dar es Salaam summit; the ASCENT program ($5 billion IDA over 4–6 years) and the Dar es Salaam Energy Declaration's forty-eight signatories) hit 50 million-plus connections by mid-2026 by its own count — and drew an ODI audit (June 2026) questioning the accounting ("outcome-accounting construct"; legacy-beneficiary migration into new counts), the standing risk for every target-driven program.

**The rivals at the gate.** The competitive context the shareholding debates ignore: the **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank** (Jin Liqun president; subscribed capital second only to the Bank's family among multilaterals) has grown from a co-financer into a parallel system — its procurement data showing Chinese firms taking the majority of its corporate contract value, the mirror image of the contractor funnel — while lending to Russia and alternative-currency experimentation proceed outside Washington's oversight. The **New Development Bank** (the BRICS bank) drifted through its 2025 crisis — Dilma Rousseff's departure in July 2025 and Anil Kishora's acting presidency — but remains the standing alternative channel for the non-aligned. The African Development Bank (Akinwumi Adesina) and the Asian Development Bank (Masato Kanda from February 2025) hold the regional middle; the MDB Heads group convenes all of them (chair rotating: IDB 2024, CEB 2025, ADB 2026). The point is not that the rivals are about to replace the Bretton Woods institutions — their combined balance sheets remain a fraction — but that the 85 percent lock now prices a real option: every "modest measures" review lengthens the queue of governments that have somewhere else to start.

# PART VIII — IS IT WORTH IT? AND HOW TO FIX IT

## 38. The Evidence Both Ways

**For the institution, in full.** The Bank's best work is real and, at its best, unmatched by any other actor:

- *Disease eradication and control.* The Onchocerciasis Control Programme (launched 1974, McNamara's era; a quarter-billion dollars of Bank financing across four decades) protected some fifty million people across West Africa from river blindness and remains the template for integrated disease control; the HIV/AIDS Multi-Country Program (2000) was the Bank's entry into the defining health fight of the era; the Bank incubated GAVI (the vaccine alliance launched from a 1998 Rockefeller summit Wolfensohn convened; GAVI's secretariat initially at UNICEF, its financing machinery — the International Finance Facility for Immunisation — still run by the Bank as treasury manager, raising over $10 billion on the bond market; GAVI's ledger: 1.1 billion children vaccinated, 18.8 million deaths averted, $250 billion in economic benefit claimed); and the COVID vaccine financing ($12 billion pledged, scaled to $20 billion; vaccine doses reaching hundreds of millions).
- *Crisis response at scale.* The 2008–10 surge ($100 billion committed; Latvia, Hungary, Mexico financed within weeks); the COVID surge ($157 billion in fifteen months by the strict count; $204 billion across the first two calendar years; safety-net systems reaching 1.1 billion people by the Bank's count); Ukraine (over $42 billion mobilized; the state kept solvent and lit through a war).
- *The empirical revolution in anti-poverty policy.* The Bank's research apparatus — the Development Research Group, the World Development Reports, the Living Standards Measurement Surveys — built the evidence base on cash transfers: Uganda's Youth Opportunities Program returning roughly 35 percent annually on capital granted (a seven-year follow-up found recipients' earnings up nearly 50 percent); no labor-supply collapse in the great majority of trials; measurable stress-biomarker improvements (cortisol) in the GiveDirectly experiments; Malawi's social cash transfer raising agricultural asset investment among the ultra-poor. The targeting literature of the good-policy era (Burnside and Dollar's "Aid, Policies, and Growth" and successors): a billion dollars of IDA in a well-governed setting moved several hundred thousand people out of extreme poverty; the policy-targeted aid of the 1990s was roughly three times as effective as untargeted aid. Ethiopia's basic-services era: under-five mortality halved in program areas; enrollment up by double digits.
- *The public goods no one else produces.* The global data commons (Part II §11); the Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) series; the catastrophe-bond shelf (Jamaica's Melissa payout as the 2026 proof-of-concept); the debt-sustainability machinery; the Inspection Panel precedent that every development bank now copies.
- *Afghanistan's carve-out.* The ARTF's NGO/UN channel after 2021 — health and education kept alive inside a Taliban state without funding the Taliban: the single most creative use of the trustee identity in the Bank's history.

**Against the institution, in full.** The iconoclasts' case, taken seriously rather than caricatured:

- *Easterly (The Elusive Quest for Growth, 2001; "The White Man's Burden," 2006):* the planners' tragedy. The average high-adjustment African country received twenty-four adjustment loans and ended with negative per-capita growth; the $2.3 trillion of aid to Africa between 1960 and 2000 left twelve-cent malaria medicines undelivered; the incentive structure — money must move, results are unverifiable — selects for announcements over outcomes. His prescription — feedback, accountability to beneficiaries, small measurable targets, searchers not planners — is the intellectual ancestor of the cash-transfer evidence and the scorecard alike.
- *Deaton (The Great Escape, 2013):* the escape from poverty is a national achievement — growth, state-building, and time — that aid mostly accompanies rather than causes; the best thing rich countries could do (open markets, stop arms sales, reform their own) costs no aid budgets and is never the proposal.
- *Moyo (Dead Aid, 2009):* aid as the debilitating drip — the fiscal-relief channel (aid substitutes for taxation, taxation substitutes for accountability) documented across Africa's aid-dependent decades.
- *The Meltzer Commission (2000):* the institutional version — the Bank is "irrelevant to its poverty mission" with a 65–70 percent failure rate in the poorest countries; recommended shrinking both Bretton Woods institutions toward their genuine comparative advantages (global public goods, crisis finance) and giving the rest back to the market. The report's Senate reception (party-line) buried it; its analytical skeleton walked into the 2023 G20 review and the Evolution Roadmap.
- *The ledger of this document:* the adjustment era's 146 protest episodes and wage-share collapses; the mega-project casebook from Narmada to Kariba; the corruption tax with names; the rating inflation that makes the success statistics themselves suspect; the pledge-gap arithmetic ($71.7 billion across two decades of African ledgers alone); and the capture pattern running from Aswan to the Board of Peace.

**The honest synthesis.** The Bank is neither the indispensable engine of development nor the neo-colonial destroyer of caricature. It is a large, slow, compromise machine that at its best delivers public goods no one else will — disease control, crisis liquidity, global data, an investor courtroom, a trustee of last resort — and at its worst finances the destruction it was built to prevent, with the ratio between the two determined by governance, not talent. The successes cluster where the Bank's comparative advantage is real (global public goods, knowledge, crisis finance) and its accountability is external (evaluated, published, replicated). The failures cluster where the volume imperative rules (mega-projects, adjustment, mission creep) and accountability is internal (rated by itself). That is fixable governance. Hence the final section: a blueprint, not an obituary.

## 39. The Blueprints That Already Exist

The striking fact of the reform literature is that no one lacks for a plan. What lacks is the 85 percent. The complete inventory, organized by problem:

**Governance.** (a) The **double-majority presidency election** (Brazil, April 2026): majority of voting power and majority of member countries — ends the appointment privilege without touching the Articles; its fate is the litmus test of the 2026–28 window. (b) Cut the amendment threshold from 85 to 80 percent — one clause, ends the single-shareholder veto on constitutional change (the Zedillo Commission's first recommendation, sixteen years stale). (c) Zedillo's menu in full: a twenty-seat fully elected board with elected chairs (ending the appointment of chairs by the president); sever the voting-share link to IMF quotas; formal five-year strategy reviews; a president selected on merit regardless of nationality. (d) the Institute for New Economic Thinking's graded scenarios (September 2025): worst case — modest alignment shifts with preemptive-right protections locking the status quo; modest — dynamic formula plus misalignment limits; best case — a Washington–Beijing grand bargain: Chinese share acceptance in exchange for capital increases and governance voice, with the European seats paying the adjustment. (e) The revolving-door brake: a five-year cooling-off between Bank senior management and the institutions whose business the Bank finances; recusal rules severing private-directorships from public duties (the Board of Peace as the negative exemplar); disclosure of all outside roles — the rule Jim Kim's board had to extract in 2018. (f) Voice secondments and a rebalanced constituency map: the Africa Group 1's 1.38 percent of votes for twenty-three countries is the arithmetic of the system's illegitimacy, and every review that produces "modest measures" deepens it.

**Money.** (a) The **trillion-dollar-bank arithmetic** (Kharas and Bhattacharya, Brookings, April 2023): triple IBRD to $80–100 billion a year of lending and a trillion-dollar loan book by 2030 using existing equity — with the honest caveats they attached: mobilization ratios must rise from the $2.6 billion of 2019 toward the G20's targets, guarantees must be designed to be called, and the below-GDI lending condition of 2018 must actually be enforced. (b) The CGD option table: a green-tinted general capital increase ($32 billion of paid-in buys roughly $100 billion of additional lending through 2030; the US share ~$6 billion); or a new intermediary entity between IBRD and IDA for global-public-goods lending (clean balance sheet, no legacy portfolio); or a climate guarantee fund (cheap, fast, leverage-heavy); or consolidation of the two dozen climate funds the system has accreted. (c) Bridgetown 3.0's eight demands (the Mottley initiative's mature form): IDA tripled by 2030 (≥$120 billion envelopes); $300 billion a year of affordable 30–50-year MDB financing; concessional eligibility extended to climate vulnerability, not just income; a fully replenished Green Climate Fund; $500 billion a year of private-capital mobilization with integrity standards; a new $650 billion SDR allocation; RST reform; universal contingent financing (disaster clauses in all lending). (d) **SDR hybrid capital** (the AfDB/IDB template, documented in the SSRN literature and the CGD tracker): rich countries lend their idle SDRs to MDBs as hybrid capital; the instruments preserve reserve-asset status in IMF statistics; leverage runs two-to-four times (SDR 100 million becomes SDR 200–400 million of loans); $400 billion of rechannelable SDRs becomes, in principle, up to $1.6 trillion of lending capacity; the CGD's caution — the AfDB has struggled to find enough donors to complete its own modest issuance — is the political-economy footnote that matters. (e) Securitization of sovereign loan portfolios (the Rockefeller Foundation-commissioned Risk Control studies, 2023): carving out 10 percent of performing portfolios into a true-sale structure yields $6–13 billion of IBRD headroom and $23–28 billion of IDA headroom — with the reputational risk priced honestly (the first loss piece must be credible). (f) Raise the IBRD→IDA transfer to 85 percent of allocable income (from the FY2024 pattern of ~$500 million against $1.85 billion of allocable income); and a 20 percent-of-portfolio guarantee target after a history of one call. (g) Fix the pledge gap mechanically: disbursement schedules attached to every announcement, published quarterly, with the Gavi-style replenishment arithmetic audited (new money versus refinancing versus reprogramming). (h) Vulnerability-based allocation: IDA eligibility that counts climate exposure and debt distress alongside income — the Bridgetown demand the Bank has begun to edge toward with the Small Island window.

**Integrity.** (a) The Vaughn/GAP package, twenty years old and still correct (the 2006 assessment scored the Bank's whistleblower policy 12 of 20 against best practice, the UN system 15 of 20): protection extended to all staff categories including consultants and former staff; reporting channels to the board and to external authorities (the current rules punish external reporting with termination); the burden of proof on the institution in retaliation cases (the US Whistleblower Protection Enhancement Act standard); make-whole remedies (reinstatement, back pay, damages); binding external arbitration as an alternative to the internal tribunal; counsel, discovery, open hearings, written decisions; and the structural fix the Hudes case screams for — INT oversight by the board, not the president, with periodic external review. (b) Beneficial-ownership transparency: mainstreamed in July 2022 (after a decade of NGO pressure); now enforce it — publish the beneficial owners of every winning bidder, and close the GAO-documented gap between registration country and real ownership. (c) Open-contracting data standards end-to-end (the STEP system's manual-entry failure is the opening); red-flag analytics run before award, not after. (d) Sanctions that recover: restitution as a standard condition with published recovery rates; the current system debars and walks away. (e) Data integrity as a board-audited function with the Doing Business post-mortem codified: who touched the data, when, and under whose instruction — logged and reviewable. (f) The tribunal, reformed: the sub-15 percent reinstatement rate and the Decision-437 one-sentence dismissals are the measure; an external appellate instance (the strike-and-mutual-consent selection the GAP proposed) would cost almost nothing.

**Delivery.** (a) Trust-fund consolidation with teeth: the Umbrella 2.0 direction, plus hard sunset clauses, single administration agreements per program, and harmonized reporting — the 748-fund peak must never recur. (b) Cash-first allocation where the evidence supports it — the 35-percent-return findings make the current infrastructure-first bias an evidence violation, not a taste. (c) The crisis toolkit scaled: every IDA country with a triggerable instrument before the next shock (74 countries today; 100 percent coverage is a budget line, not a discovery). (d) Independent verification generalized (PforR's verifier model across all results claims). (e) Country-level CPIA scores published — the allocation formula's inputs should not be state secrets. (f) Preparation support for the borrowers the system starves: the 11.8-month Bangladesh lag and the 5.5-year IDA disbursement average are capacity failures the Bank can fix with trust-funded implementation units — the one trust-fund use nobody argues with.

**Climate, honestly.** Independent verification of climate claims (the Oxfam critique answered, not waved off); grant shares restored for adaptation (the 9 percent grant share is the real scandal, not the counting method); the Green Equity Approach extended to oil and gas; the bond-underwriting and captive-coal loopholes closed (the 68.3-gigawatt finding demands it); natural-disaster clauses in all new lending (Bridgetown's demand, half-met); and the 2026 retreat reversed on the evidence of the independent evaluation the board has already ordered — with the 19-director letter as the standing coalition to implement it.

**The verdict.** Worth it? Run the arithmetic both ways. The institution costs the world about $3.5 billion a year to run (its net administrative budget) and returned, in its best recent year alone, $161.9 billion of commitments, a $51 billion climate portfolio, the closest thing the poor have to a lender of last resort, and the data commons everyone else free-rides on. Against: the adjustment-era ledger, the casebook, the corruption tax, and the capture pattern — harms with names and dates that no aggregate can amortize. The synthesis this document has built toward: the Bank's failures are governance failures with fingerprints, and the blueprint above is the public record of how to reverse each one — every proposal named, sourced, and costed, most of them two decades old. What stands between the blueprint and the institution is not knowledge — it is 15.9 percent of the votes, which is either the final argument for reform or, if held long enough, the slow argument for replacement by whatever the BRICS institutions, the Board of Peace, and the bilateralists build next. The borrowers, who have no votes in that debate either, will take whichever arrives with money.
